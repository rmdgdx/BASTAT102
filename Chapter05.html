🧠 The one sentence this chapter exists to install

The confidence level belongs to the procedure, not to the interval in front of you. Once you have computed [303.18, 320.82], μ is either inside it or it is not — there is no probability left to speak of. The 95% describes how often a method like this one succeeds across all the samples you might have drawn. Nearly every misuse of a confidence interval in business is a version of forgetting that sentence.

5.1From a point estimate to an interval estimate

A business process outsourcing firm in Ortigas measures the average handle time of a random sample of 100 customer calls and gets 312 seconds. The operations director asks: "So what's our average handle time?"

The tempting answer is "312 seconds." It is also, almost certainly, wrong. Chapter 4 established why: x̄ is a random variable. Draw a different 100 calls and you get 309, or 316, or 314.5. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator, which means it is centred on μ — but "centred on" is not "equal to", and no single sample mean has any real chance of landing exactly on the population mean.

📖 Two kinds of estimate

A point estimate is a single number offered as the value of a parameter. x̄ estimates μ, s estimates σ, p̂ estimates p. It is the best single guess you have, and it comes with no admission of error.

An interval estimate is a range of values, computed from the sample, within which the parameter is believed to lie — together with a stated level of confidence. It says the same thing as a point estimate and tells you how much to trust it.

Why the point estimate is not enough

Consider two research reports that both state an average customer spend of ₱3,850:

ReportWhat it saysWhat a manager can do with it
A · point estimate only"Average spend is ₱3,850."Nothing safely. Is the true figure ₱3,840 or ₱1,200? The report has not said, and the reader has no way to tell.
B · interval estimate"Average spend is ₱3,850, 95% CI [₱3,591, ₱4,109]."A great deal. A pricing model that breaks below ₱3,500 is safe. One that breaks below ₱4,000 is not. The decision follows directly.

Report B contains strictly more information, costs nothing extra to produce, and is the professional standard in every field that takes evidence seriously — medicine, economics, market research, quality engineering. A point estimate published without a measure of its uncertainty is not a finding; it is an assertion.

💼 Where you have already seen this

Election polling reports "42% ± 3 percentage points." Unemployment statistics come with standard errors. Clinical trials report "a 23% reduction in risk (95% CI: 11% to 34%)." Manufacturing tolerances are intervals by construction. The interval estimate is not an academic refinement — it is how quantitative evidence is normally communicated everywhere outside a badly written business report.

The shape every interval in this chapter has

Before any formula, learn the skeleton. Every confidence interval you will build in this course — and most that you will build afterwards — has exactly this form:

point estimate  ±  ( multiplier  ×  standard error )

The three pieces do three different jobs, and keeping them apart is most of the battle:

📍 The point estimate

Where the interval is centred: x̄, p̂, or whatever statistic answers the question. It comes from your data and nothing else.

✖️ The multiplier

How many standard errors wide to reach. It comes from the confidence level you chose and the distribution that applies — z or t.

📐 The standard error

The typical distance between the statistic and the parameter. It comes from the variability of the data and the sample size. This is §4.6 doing its work.

The quantity multiplier × standard error has its own name: the margin of error, usually written E. So an equivalent way to write the skeleton is estimate ± E, and the whole of §5.7 will be about choosing n so that E comes out where you need it.

⚠️ One thing the skeleton does not contain

There is no term anywhere in estimate ± multiplier × SE for a biased sample, a bad frame or a 30% response rate. Chapter 4's standing precondition still stands, and it will stand for the rest of the course: these formulas presume a probability sample from the population you intend to describe. An interval computed on a convenience sample is arithmetic, not evidence.

🏁 30-second recap

A point estimate is one number with no admission of error; an interval estimate adds a range and a confidence level. Every interval in this chapter is estimate ± (multiplier × standard error), and the product of the last two is the margin of error E. None of it survives a non-probability sample.

5.2What "95% confident" actually means

This section contains no arithmetic and is the most important one in the chapter. The confidence interval is the most widely used and most widely misunderstood object in applied statistics, and the misunderstanding is always the same.

Here is the interval from §5.1, computed properly: the 95% confidence interval for mean handle time is [303.18 s, 320.82 s]. Now consider four sentences a manager might write about it.

Why "95% probability" is the wrong phrase

The tempting statement is: "There is a 95% probability that μ lies between 303.18 and 320.82." The problem is that μ is not random. The population mean handle time is a fixed number — unknown to you, but fixed. It is 314.6, or 308.2, or whatever it is, and it does not fluctuate.

Once you have computed a specific interval from a specific sample, both endpoints are also fixed numbers. So the statement "μ is between 303.18 and 320.82" is either true or false. There is no probability left in it, in the same way that "the population of Manila exceeds one million" is not a 95% probability statement — it is simply true.

🔁 What is random

Before you draw the sample, the interval you are about to compute is random, because x̄ is random. Different samples produce different intervals — some further left, some further right, all of the same width. 95% of those possible intervals contain μ. You draw one of them. You do not know which kind you got. The confidence level describes the reliability of the machine, not the contents of the one item it just produced.

🏭 An analogy that survives scrutiny

A machine fills 500 ml bottles and gets it right 95% of the time. You take one bottle off the line. Is there a "95% probability" this bottle is correctly filled? Loosely, yes — but the bottle is already filled. Its volume is a fixed fact; the 95% is a property of the machine. That is exactly the relationship between a confidence level and a confidence interval. The interval is the bottle; the procedure is the machine.

The three misreadings, named

The misreadingWhat it confusesHow to catch yourself
"95% probability that μ is in here"Treats a fixed parameter as a random variableAsk: what exactly is varying? If the answer is "the interval", say so. The correct phrasing is "we are 95% confident", where confident is a technical term meaning "produced by a method that works 95% of the time".
"95% of customers fall in this range"The spread of the mean (σ/√n) with the spread of the data (σ)Compare widths. This interval is 17.6 s wide; individual calls have σ = 45 s, so 95% of calls span roughly 180 s. If your interval is narrow, it cannot possibly be describing individuals.

✍️ The sentence to use in a report

"We estimate mean handle time at 312 seconds. A 95% confidence interval runs from 303 to 321 seconds, meaning that a method like this one produces an interval containing the true average in 95 of every 100 studies." That sentence is correct, is comprehensible to a non-statistician, and takes eleven seconds longer to write than the wrong one.

🚫 A common defence that does not work

Students sometimes argue: "But surely, given the data, 95% probability is a reasonable belief about μ?" That is a Bayesian statement, and Bayesian statistics does produce intervals — called credible intervals — that legitimately support exactly that reading. But they are computed differently, they require a prior distribution, and they are not what this chapter constructs. Using the Bayesian sentence to describe a frequentist interval is borrowing an interpretation that the arithmetic has not paid for. In an exam it is marked wrong; in practice it usually leads to overconfidence.

🏁 30-second recap

μ is fixed; the interval is what varies. "95% confident" means the method captures μ 95% of the time, not that this interval has a 95% probability of containing it. The three standard misreadings are the probability statement, confusing the interval with the range of the data, and confusing it with a prediction about the next sample.

5.3Estimating a mean when σ is known: the z-interval

Start with the simplest case, partly because it is the cleanest illustration of the skeleton and partly because it is the one that almost never occurs in practice — which is itself worth knowing.

x̄  ±  zα/2 · ( σ / √n )

Every symbol has already been met. x̄ is the sample mean, σ/√n is the standard error from §4.6, and zα/2 is the multiplier that cuts off α/2 in each tail of the standard normal. The subscript is worth decoding once: if the confidence level is 95%, then α = 0.05 is the total error probability, split into two tails of 0.025 each — hence z0.025 = 1.96.

☎️ Worked example — average handle time

A BPO has monitored handle time for years and the process standard deviation is stable at σ = 45 seconds. A random sample of n = 100 calls this month gives x̄ = 312 seconds. Construct 90%, 95% and 99% confidence intervals for the true mean handle time.

Step 1 · the standard error SE = σ/√n = 45/√100 = 45/10 = 4.50 s Step 2 · apply the multiplier for each level 90%: 312 ± 1.645(4.50) = 312 ± 7.40 → [304.60, 319.40]   width 14.80 95%: 312 ± 1.960(4.50) = 312 ± 8.82 → [303.18, 320.82]   width 17.64 99%: 312 ± 2.576(4.50) = 312 ± 11.59 → [300.41, 323.59]   width 23.18

Reading it back to the director. "Our best estimate of average handle time is 312 seconds, and we are 95% confident the true average lies between 303 and 321 seconds." If the service-level agreement caps average handle time at 330 seconds, the entire interval sits below the cap, at every confidence level shown — the process is comfortably compliant, and that conclusion is now backed by a stated level of evidence rather than by a single number.

🚧 Notice what raising confidence did

Moving from 90% to 99% widened the interval from 14.8 s to 23.2 s — a 57% increase — on exactly the same data. You cannot buy certainty for free: the only currency a confidence interval accepts is width. This is the trade §5.9 is about, and it is why "let's use 99% to be safe" is not automatically the professional choice. A 99% interval that spans the entire range of defensible business decisions is safer and useless.

🤔 When is σ ever actually known?

Rarely, and always for the same reason: a long-established, tightly monitored process whose variability has been measured over thousands of observations. Manufacturing tolerances on a mature production line. Standardised test scores where the scale was designed to have a fixed σ. Instrument measurement error quoted by the manufacturer. Call-handling time in a BPO with years of logged data, as above. Notice what these have in common: σ was estimated once from an enormous amount of data and then treated as a known constant. In a fresh market-research study, a pilot survey, or any first look at a new process, σ is unknown — and reaching for this formula anyway is the single most common error the next section exists to prevent.

🏁 30-second recap

The z-interval is x̄ ± z(σ/√n). Multipliers: 1.645 / 1.960 / 2.576 for 90 / 95 / 99%. Worked case: x̄ = 312, σ = 45, n = 100 → SE 4.50 → 95% CI [303.18, 320.82]. Raising confidence widens the interval, and the formula requires that σ be genuinely known — which it usually is not.

5.4The t distribution: what it costs not to know σ

In real work you compute s from your sample and use it in place of σ. That substitution is not free, and the t distribution is the price list.

Here is the difficulty. When σ is known, only one quantity in (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) is random: x̄. That ratio follows a standard normal distribution, and 1.96 is the right multiplier. When you replace σ with s, two quantities are random — x̄ and s — because s is itself an estimate that changes from sample to sample. The extra wobble makes extreme values more likely than the normal curve predicts, so a multiplier read off the normal curve would be too small and the interval too narrow.

📖 The Student t distribution

Published in 1908 by William Sealy Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, under the pen name "Student" because his employer forbade staff from publishing. His problem was practical and thoroughly commercial: assessing barley and hops from small samples, where n was a dozen rather than a thousand and the normal approximation demonstrably failed.

The distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped like the normal, centred at zero, but with heavier tails. Its exact shape is set by one parameter, the degrees of freedom, which for a one-sample interval is df = n − 1.

Why n − 1? The degrees-of-freedom idea

You met the n − 1 in the sample variance formula in Chapter 2 without a full explanation. Here it is. Degrees of freedom counts how many values in a calculation are free to vary once the constraints are imposed.

🔢 A concrete demonstration

Suppose five branch sales figures have a mean of ₱200,000. Choose the first four freely — say ₱180k, ₱220k, ₱190k, ₱240k. Now the fifth is forced: the total must be ₱1,000,000, so the fifth must be ₱170,000. You had five numbers but only four free choices.

The sample variance is computed around x̄, which is itself estimated from the same data. That one estimated quantity consumes one degree of freedom, leaving n − 1. It is also why dividing by n − 1 rather than n makes s² an unbiased estimator of σ²: dividing by n would systematically underestimate, because the deviations are measured from x̄ — the value that makes them as small as they can possibly be — rather than from the unknown μ.

Reading a t-table

A t-table is indexed by degrees of freedom down the side and by tail area across the top. Three habits prevent most errors:

1️⃣ Compute df first

df = n − 1. Write it down before opening the table. Looking up row n instead of row n − 1 is the most common table error and always makes the interval too narrow.

2️⃣ Halve the α

A 95% two-sided interval needs the column for 0.025 in one tail, not 0.05. Many tables label columns by one-tail area; some by two-tail. Check which one you are holding.

3️⃣ Sanity-check against z

Any two-sided 95% t value must be larger than 1.96 and must shrink as df grows. If you have read off something below 1.96, you are in the wrong column.

⚠️ The assumption t carries that z did not

The z-interval needed the sampling distribution of x̄ to be normal, which the CLT delivers for large n whatever the population looks like. The t-interval needs something stronger when n is small: the population itself should be approximately normal, or at least not severely skewed and free of extreme outliers. The reason is that t's derivation assumes s is behaving predictably, which it does not do in a skewed population at small n. At n ≥ 30 the procedure is robust and mild departures do not matter; at n = 8 from a heavily skewed population, a t-interval is not trustworthy. Check with a histogram or a boxplot before you compute — and if the shape is bad, say so rather than producing a number.

🏁 30-second recap

Replacing σ with s adds a second source of randomness, so the multiplier comes from the t distribution with df = n − 1, not the normal. t has heavier tails, so its multiplier is always larger than z, and it converges to z as df grows (2.262 at df = 9, 2.045 at df = 29, 1.972 at df = 199). Small-n t-intervals additionally assume an approximately normal population.

5.5Estimating a mean when σ is unknown: the t-interval

This is the workhorse. Nine times in ten, the interval a business analyst actually builds is this one.

x̄  ±  tα/2, n−1 · ( s / √n )

Structurally identical to the z-interval, with two substitutions: s replaces σ, and t with n − 1 degrees of freedom replaces z. Everything else — the centring, the two-tail split, the interpretation — is unchanged.

🍗 Worked example — franchise food cost, computed by hand

A quick-service restaurant franchisor audits food cost as a percentage of sales at a random sample of 15 of its 420 branches. Head office wants to know the chain-wide average, because the franchise agreement is written around a 33% target. The 15 figures:

31.2   33.5   30.8   34.1   32.6   29.8   35.2   33.0   31.7   34.8   32.2   30.4   33.9   32.8   31.5

Step 1 · the sample mean Σx = 487.5    x̄ = 487.5 / 15 = 32.50% Step 2 · the sample standard deviation Σx² = 15,880.21    (Σx)²/n = 487.5²/15 = 237,656.25/15 = 15,843.75 SS = Σx² − (Σx)²/n = 15,880.21 − 15,843.75 = 36.46 s² = SS/(n−1) = 36.46/14 = 2.604286    s = √2.604286 = 1.6138% Step 3 · the standard error SE = s/√n = 1.6138/√15 = 1.6138/3.87298 = 0.41668 Step 4 · the multiplier — df = 15 − 1 = 14, two-tailed 95% t0.025, 14 = 2.145   (compare z = 1.960 — the small-sample premium is 9.4%) Step 5 · assemble E = 2.145 × 0.41668 = 0.8937 95% CI = 32.50 ± 0.89 → [31.61%, 33.39%]

Interpreting it against the business question

The franchisor's target is 33.0%, and 33.0 falls inside the interval [31.61, 33.39]. The correct conclusion is careful and worth stating precisely:

🎯 What the interval licenses you to say

Defensible: "We estimate chain-wide food cost at 32.5% of sales, 95% CI [31.6%, 33.4%]. The 33% contractual target lies inside that interval, so this sample gives no clear evidence that the chain is running above or below target."

Not defensible: "Food cost is 32.5%, so we are 0.5 points under target and should relax." The sample cannot distinguish 32.5% from 33.2% — both are entirely consistent with the data. Acting on the point estimate alone treats a difference the study cannot detect as though it were established fact.

🚫 What using z instead of t would have done

With z = 1.96 the margin would be 1.96 × 0.41668 = 0.8167, giving [31.68, 33.32] — a width of 1.633 instead of 1.787, or 8.6% too narrow. In this case the business conclusion happens not to change. That is luck, not method: the error runs in the dangerous direction (overstating precision) and it grows fast as n falls — 17% too narrow at n = 8, 29% at n = 5. Since checking df and reading the t-table costs about twenty seconds, there is no situation in which the shortcut is worth taking.

Checking the assumption before you trust the number

At n = 15 the t-interval leans on the population being roughly normal. Sort the data and look:

29.8   30.4   30.8   31.2   31.5   31.7   32.2   32.6   32.8   33.0   33.5   33.9   34.1   34.8   35.2

The median is 32.6 against a mean of 32.50 — close, so no strong skew. The range runs 5.4 points, symmetric either side of the centre (2.8 below, 2.6 above). No value sits far from the pack. This is exactly what a small sample from an approximately normal population looks like, and the t-interval is safe to report.

🧪 Try it — the same audit, one branch different

Suppose the 29.8 had instead been 18.4 — one branch with an unusual supply arrangement. Recompute x̄ and s. You will find the mean drops to 31.74 and s rises to 3.96, so the interval widens to roughly [29.55, 33.93] — more than twice as wide, driven by a single observation. That is the small-sample sensitivity the normality assumption is warning you about. The right response is not a wider interval but an investigation: is that branch a data error, a genuinely different business, or the first sign that the population is not what you assumed?

The decision rule, in one line

🚦 z or t?

If the problem hands you σ, use z. If it hands you s, use t. That is the whole rule, and it is right essentially always. Some older texts add "use z if n ≥ 30 even when σ is unknown" — a workaround from the era of printed tables, when t-tables stopped at df = 30. It is harmless because t and z are then nearly identical, but it is not the modern practice, and if you use t whenever σ is unknown you will never be wrong.

🏁 30-second recap

The t-interval is x̄ ± tα/2, n−1(s/√n), and it is the everyday case. Worked example: n = 15, x̄ = 32.50%, s = 1.6138, SE = 0.41668, t = 2.145 → [31.61%, 33.39%], which contains the 33% target, so no difference is demonstrated. Using z would have made the interval 8.6% too narrow. Check the shape of the data before trusting a small-sample t-interval.

5.6Estimating a proportion

Half the questions business asks are proportions: what share will buy, what share will churn, what share of units are defective. The interval has the same skeleton, and one genuinely new wrinkle.

p̂  ±  zα/2 · √[ p̂(1 − p̂) / n ]

Two features distinguish it from the mean case. First, z is used, never t. The reason is worth stating precisely, because the usual one-liner is wrong: it is not that nothing is being estimated in the standard error — p̂ appears there and p̂ is certainly random. It is that the variance of p̂ is a function of p itself, so estimating it consumes no separate parameter and there is no degrees-of-freedom adjustment to make. This interval is a large-sample normal approximation with no t-style small-sample correction available, which is why what protects it is the success/failure condition below rather than a change of multiplier. (Exact small-sample methods for a proportion do exist — the Wilson and Clopper–Pearson intervals — but they are a different construction, asymmetric by design, rather than the same formula with a bigger multiplier.) Second, the standard error is built from p̂ in place of the unknown p, which is legitimate provided the sample is large enough for p̂ to be a good stand-in.

✅ The condition to check first, every time
n·p̂ ≥ 5   and   n(1 − p̂) ≥ 5

In words: you need at least about five successes and five failures. Both must hold. Like the n ≥ 30 rule this is a convention rather than a theorem — Agresti and several other texts require 10 or even 15 for interval work — so treat a borderline case with an extreme p̂ as a reason for caution rather than a pass. When the condition genuinely fails, the symmetric interval can run below 0 or above 1, which is a visible signal that the method has broken.

📱 Worked example — e-wallet adoption

A retail chain surveys a random sample of 600 customers and finds that 384 used an e-wallet to pay on their most recent visit. Estimate the true adoption rate with 95% confidence.

Step 1 · the point estimate p̂ = 384/600 = 0.64  (64%) Step 2 · check the condition np̂ = 384 ✓    n(1−p̂) = 216 ✓    both far above 5 Step 3 · the standard error SE = √[0.64 × 0.36 / 600] = √[0.2304/600] = √0.000384 = 0.019596 Step 4 · the margin and the interval E = 1.96 × 0.019596 = 0.038407  →  3.84 percentage points 95% CI = 0.64 ± 0.0384 → [0.6016, 0.6784]  →  60.2% to 67.8%
📏 Percentage points, not percent

The margin here is ±3.84 percentage points, not "±3.84%". The distinction matters: 3.84% of 64% would be 2.46 points, a different and much narrower claim. Percentage points measure the arithmetic gap between two percentages; percent measures a relative change. Mixing them is a reporting error that survives into press releases with depressing regularity.

Reading the interval like a decision-maker

Suppose the chain's payment-systems investment only pays back if adoption exceeds 55%. The entire interval [60.2%, 67.8%] sits above 55%, so the investment case is supported at 95% confidence. Now suppose instead the threshold were 65%. The interval straddles it, so the honest report is that the data cannot resolve whether adoption is above or below the threshold — and the correct managerial response is either to collect more data or to make the decision robust to both possibilities, not to round 64% up and proceed.

🔗 The link back to Chapter 4

If the sample is a large share of a finite population, the finite population correction from §4.9 applies here too: multiply the standard error by √[(N−n)/(N−1)] whenever n/N > 5%. With 600 customers drawn from a base of 40,000 the fraction is 1.5% and the correction is ignored. With 600 drawn from a base of 4,000 it is 15%, the correction is 0.9221, and the margin narrows from ±3.84 to ±3.54 percentage points — free precision that a careless analyst leaves on the table.

🏁 30-second recap

The proportion interval is p̂ ± z√[p̂(1−p̂)/n], always with z and never with t. Check np̂ ≥ 5 and n(1−p̂) ≥ 5 first. Worked example: 384/600 → p̂ = 0.64, SE = 0.019596, margin ±3.84 percentage points, CI [60.2%, 67.8%]. Apply the fpc if n/N exceeds 5%.

5.7Determining the sample size before you collect anything

Every formula so far has taken n as given and produced a margin of error. Research planning runs the other way: management states the margin it needs, and you solve for n. This is the most commercially useful algebra in the course.

Sample size for a mean

Start from the margin of error and rearrange. Since E = z(σ/√n), multiply both sides by √n, divide by E, and square:

n  =  ( zα/2 · σ / E )²
☎️ Worked example — planning next quarter's audit

The BPO's σ = 45 seconds. Operations wants a 95% margin of error on mean handle time of ±10 s, ±5 s and ±2 s. How many calls must be sampled in each case?

±10 s: n = (1.96 × 45 / 10)² = (8.82)² = 77.79 → n = 78 ±5 s: n = (1.96 × 45 / 5)² = (17.64)² = 311.17 → n = 312 ±2 s: n = (1.96 × 45 / 2)² = (44.10)² = 1,944.81 → n = 1,945 Always round UP. Rounding down leaves the margin wider than the one you promised.
🐔 The chicken-and-egg problem, and the three ways out

The formula needs σ, but σ is what a study measures. Three legitimate sources:

  1. Historical data or a prior study. The best option when it exists, and the one used above.
  2. A pilot study. Run 20–30 observations, compute s, use it as σ. Costs a little, and often surfaces questionnaire problems worth more than the σ.
  3. The range rule. If you know the plausible minimum and maximum, σ ≈ range/4, since roughly 95% of a normal distribution spans four standard deviations. Crude, but far better than guessing, and defensible in a proposal when nothing else exists.
⚙️ A refinement worth knowing

The formula uses z, but if σ is unknown the eventual interval will use t — and t is larger, so the realised margin will be slightly wider than planned. Example: with s ≈ 620 and a target of ±₱200, the z formula gives n = (1.96 × 620/200)² = 36.92 → 37. But at n = 37, df = 36 and t = 2.028, so the realised margin is 2.028 × 620/√37 = ₱206.72 — over target. Iterating: n = 40 gives ₱198.29 ✓ and n = 41 gives ₱195.70. The practical rule is to add about 10% to the z-based n whenever σ is unknown — here 37 × 1.1 ≈ 41 — and to add more on top for anticipated non-response.

Sample size for a proportion

n  =  p̂(1 − p̂) · ( zα/2 / E )²

Same rearrangement, applied to the proportion standard error. And it has the same chicken-and-egg problem in a sharper form: you need p to plan the study that measures p. Here the way out is elegant.

Target ±3 percentage points at 95% confidence No prior information, use p = 0.5: n = 0.25 × (1.96/0.03)² = 0.25 × 4,268.4 = 1,067.1 → n = 1,068 Prior study suggests p̂ ≈ 0.64: n = 0.64 × 0.36 × (1.96/0.03)² = 0.2304 × 4,268.4 = 983.4 → n = 984 Using the prior estimate saves 84 respondents — about 8% of the fieldwork budget. Worth having, but not worth inventing.
🗳️ Where the famous "n = 1,000" comes from

National opinion polls almost universally report a margin of about ±3 points and a sample near 1,000. Now you can see why: 1,068 is the conservative n for ±3 points at 95% confidence, and the √n relationship means going to ±2 points would need 2,401 while ±1 point would need 9,604. Somewhere around 1,000 the marginal precision stops being worth the marginal peso — and that judgement, not the arithmetic, is the professional skill.

🎯 The question that comes before the formula

"How precise do we need to be?" is a business question, not a statistical one, and it is answered by asking what decision the number feeds. If the board will launch the product when adoption exceeds 55% and current estimates hover near 64%, a ±5-point margin is ample and ±1 point is waste. If the threshold were 63%, ±5 points would be useless. Work backwards from the decision to the precision to the sample size — never forwards from the budget to a sample size to whatever margin falls out.

🏁 30-second recap

For a mean, n = (zσ/E)²; for a proportion, n = p̂(1−p̂)(z/E)². Always round up. σ comes from history, a pilot, or range/4; p defaults to 0.5, which maximises √[p(1−p)] and so guarantees sufficiency. Add about 10% when σ is unknown and more for non-response. Decide the precision the decision needs before touching the formula.

5.8Estimating a variance or a standard deviation

Sometimes the business question is not "how much on average?" but "how consistent?" — and consistency is a variance question. This interval behaves differently from every other one in the chapter, in a way worth understanding rather than memorising.

🎯 When spread is the thing you care about
🏭ManufacturingA filling line averaging exactly 500 ml is worthless if individual bottles range from 460 to 540. Tolerance is a variance specification.
⏱️Service deliveryTwo couriers both average 30 minutes; one delivers every parcel in 28–32, the other in 12–55. Customers experience the second as unreliable.
📈FinanceVolatility is the standard deviation of returns. An interval around it is an interval around the risk itself.
✅Supplier assessmentA supplier who claims "our standard deviation is under 3 grams" has made a testable statistical claim, and this is the test.

Why this one needs a different distribution

Sample means are approximately normally distributed, which is why z and t work. Sample variances are not. The quantity s² is built from squared deviations, so it can never be negative, and it has a long right tail — an unusual sample can inflate s² a great deal but can only deflate it as far as zero. The distribution that describes it is the chi-square distribution, χ², with df = n − 1.

( n − 1 ) s² / σ²  ~  χ²n−1

Rearranging that statement to put σ² in the middle gives the interval — and because the chi-square distribution is asymmetric, the two cut-offs are not mirror images and the two bounds use different values:

( n − 1 ) s² / χ²right  <  σ²  <  ( n − 1 ) s² / χ²left
🔀 The step that catches everyone

The larger chi-square value goes in the denominator of the lower bound, and the smaller one in the denominator of the upper bound. Dividing by a bigger number gives a smaller result — so the crossover is arithmetically inevitable, not a convention. If your "lower" bound comes out above your "upper" bound, you have used the cut-offs the right way round and then labelled them backwards.

🥤 Worked example — fill-volume consistency

A beverage plant samples 20 bottles from a filling line and finds a sample standard deviation of 4.2 ml. The equipment supplier's specification promises a process standard deviation of no more than 5 ml. Estimate σ with 95% confidence.

Step 1 · degrees of freedom and the two cut-offs df = 20 − 1 = 19 χ²left (0.025 in the lower tail) = 8.907    χ²right (0.025 in the upper tail) = 32.852 Step 2 · the numerator (n − 1)s² = 19 × 4.2² = 19 × 17.64 = 335.16 Step 3 · the interval for the variance lower: 335.16 / 32.852 = 10.202    upper: 335.16 / 8.907 = 37.629 95% CI for σ²: [10.202, 37.629] ml² Step 4 · take square roots for the standard deviation 95% CI for σ: [3.194, 6.134] ml

The business reading. The point estimate of 4.2 ml is inside the supplier's 5 ml specification, which looks reassuring. The interval says otherwise: values up to 6.13 ml are entirely consistent with these 20 bottles. The sample does not establish that the line meets specification, and a plant manager who reported "4.2, within spec" would be reporting a point estimate as though it were a finding. The correct action is to sample more bottles — and note that this is precisely the situation §5.7 exists for.

📐 Two features to notice

The interval is not symmetric around s. The estimate 4.2 sits 1.01 above the lower bound but 1.93 below the upper. There is no "s ± something" form, and writing one is wrong.

It is wide. Twenty observations pin the mean down fairly well but say remarkably little about the spread. Estimating variability well takes considerably more data than estimating a centre — a fact that repeatedly surprises people commissioning quality studies.

⚠️ The strictest assumption in the chapter

The chi-square interval requires the population to be normally distributed, and unlike the t-interval it is not robust to departures from that. A large n does not rescue it: the CLT applies to sample means, not to sample variances. With skewed or heavy-tailed data this interval can be badly wrong at any sample size. Always examine the distribution's shape first, and if it is not approximately normal, use a different tool — bootstrap intervals are the standard modern answer, and they are beyond this course but worth knowing exists.

🏁 30-second recap

Use (n−1)s²/χ² with two different cut-offs, larger in the lower bound. Worked example: n = 20, s = 4.2, df = 19, cut-offs 8.907 and 32.852 → σ² in [10.202, 37.629], σ in [3.194, 6.134] ml. The interval is asymmetric, wide, and requires an approximately normal population — an assumption a large n does not repair.

5.9The three levers on interval width

Whatever the parameter, the margin of error is multiplier × standard error, and only three things can change it. Knowing which two you control — and at what price — is what turns the formula into a management tool.

Lever 1 · The confidence level

Raising confidence means reaching further into the tails, so the multiplier grows and the interval widens. This is the lever managers most often reach for, and usually in the wrong direction.

🚫 "Let's use 99% to be safe" — usually wrong

A wider interval is not a more accurate one. It is a less informative one that is right more often. Taken to its limit, "we are 100% confident average handle time is between 0 and 600 seconds" is unimpeachable and worthless. Raise the confidence level when the cost of being wrong is severe and asymmetric — a safety limit, a regulatory threshold, a financial covenant. Otherwise 95% is the professional default precisely because it balances the two failure modes, and departing from it should be justified in the report rather than assumed.

Lever 2 · The variability of the data

A larger σ or s means a wider interval, because there is genuinely more uncertainty about where the centre sits. This lever is mostly not yours to pull: σ is a property of the population, and no amount of analysis reduces it. Two legitimate ways to move it exist, and both change the study rather than the arithmetic:

🎯 Narrow the population

Estimating mean spend across all customers mixes wholesale and retail. Estimating it within each segment gives two tighter intervals, each about a more useful quantity. This is stratification from §4.2 paying off again.

📏 Measure better

Part of observed variability is real and part is measurement noise — vague questions, inconsistent recording, unclear definitions. Reducing the second shrinks s without touching the underlying population.

Lever 3 · The sample size

The one lever fully under your control, and the one with the least favourable exchange rate. Because the standard error carries √n, the return diminishes quadratically.

✍️ Reflect

A marketing director says: "The ±4-point margin is too wide. Can you tighten it without spending more?" Three responses are available and only one is honest. You could lower the confidence level to 90%, which narrows the interval by 16% while quietly increasing the error rate from 1-in-20 to 1-in-10. You could stratify or improve measurement, which is real work with real gains. Or you could say that ±4 points is what this budget buys, and ask what decision the number feeds — because if the threshold is far from the estimate, ±4 is already good enough. Which of the three would you put in the email, and what would you say about the first one?

🏁 30-second recap

Width is driven by confidence (higher → wider, at an accelerating price), variability (mostly fixed; narrow the population or measure better), and n (quarter the width costs sixteen times the sample). A wider interval is not more accurate — it is less informative and more often right.

5.10Choosing, reporting and reading intervals

The arithmetic in this chapter is a few lines long. The professional skill is choosing the right formula and then saying what the answer means without overclaiming.

The reporting checklist

A confidence interval reported without its context is only slightly better than a point estimate. Five things belong in every report:

ElementExampleWhy it matters
The point estimate"Mean handle time 312 s"The reader's anchor, and the number that will be quoted onward whatever else you write.
The interval and its level"95% CI [303, 321] s"An interval without a stated confidence level is uninterpretable — 95% and 99% intervals look identical on the page.

📝 A model paragraph

"From a random sample of 600 customers (100% completion, drawn from the loyalty database covering 92% of transactions), e-wallet adoption is estimated at 64.0%, with a 95% confidence interval of 60.2% to 67.8% — a margin of ±3.8 percentage points. It does not, however, distinguish adoption above from below 65%, so the premium-tier forecast should not be built on the point estimate alone."

Reading someone else's interval

Most of the intervals you meet in a career will be other people's. Four questions expose almost every problem:

1️⃣ Is a confidence level stated?

If not, the interval is uninterpretable. A surprising number of reports omit it, and a few quote a 90% interval while implying 95%.

2️⃣ Is the sample a probability sample?

If the report describes a panel, a web poll or "customers who responded", the margin of error is decorative. Ask for the response rate.

3️⃣ Does the width look plausible?

An interval narrower than the standard error would allow, or one containing impossible values such as a negative spend, signals a formula error — usually s used where s/√n belonged, or the reverse.

4️⃣ Is the conclusion bigger than the interval?

"Adoption has risen" from an interval that comfortably contains last year's figure is the most common overclaim in business reporting, and the one Chapter 6 exists to discipline.

🔗 Where this goes next

Notice what you did in §5.5: the target of 33% fell inside the interval, so you declined to claim a difference. In §5.8 the 5 ml specification also fell inside, so you declined again. That reasoning — compare a claimed value with an interval, and see whether the data can rule it out — is hypothesis testing, arrived at from the other direction. Choose the formula by asking what parameter you are estimating and what you were given (σ, s, p̂, or spread itself). Report the estimate, the interval with its level, n, the design and the decision implication. 🚦 The decision cards

🎯 σ or s?

The single question that routes half this chapter. If the problem gives you σ, use z. If it gives you s, use t with df = n − 1. There is no sample size at which "given s, use z" becomes correct — it merely becomes harmless.

🗣️ The sentence that is correct

"We are 95% confident that μ lies between A and B" — where confident means the method captures μ in 95 of every 100 studies. Not "there is a 95% probability". μ is fixed; the interval is what varies.

🚫 The interval is not the data

A CI for the mean describes where μ sits, not where individual observations sit. If σ = 45 and the interval is 18 wide, it obviously cannot be describing individuals. Compare widths whenever you feel the temptation.

📈 Wider ≠ better

Raising confidence widens the interval and makes it less informative. A 100% interval is always correct and always useless. Use 95% unless the cost of being wrong is severe and asymmetric, and say why in the report.

🔢 Round up, always

A sample-size calculation of 311.17 becomes 312, and 1,067.1 becomes 1,068. Rounding down leaves the realised margin wider than the one you promised — the one direction the calculation exists to prevent.

🔗 Contains the claimed value?

If a target, specification or last year's figure falls inside the interval, the data cannot distinguish it from your estimate — so do not claim a difference. That comparison is hypothesis testing in embryo.

A · The wrong multiplier — z used where t belonged.

The memo states plainly that σ is unknown and s = ₱620 was computed from the sample. That routes the problem to the t distribution with df = 18 − 1 = 17, not to z.

Consequence. The memo overstates the precision of its own estimate by about seven percent. In this case the business conclusion is unlikely to flip, because both intervals are wide and both comfortably contain the same range of plausible values — but that is luck, not method. The error runs in the dangerous direction: it makes the study look better than it is, and the same mistake understates the width by 17% at n = 8 and 29% at n = 5. The professional point is that checking df and reading a t-table costs about twenty seconds, so there is no sample size at which the shortcut is worth taking.

Note also what the correct interval actually says: category spend could plausibly be anywhere from ₱1,177 to ₱1,793 — a range spanning ±21% of the estimate, with the top of the interval 52% above the bottom. Eighteen store-months is simply not many, and any revenue projection built on the point estimate of ₱1,485 alone is projecting from the middle of a very wide range. Note also that the sampling fraction is 18/552 = 3.3%, below the 5% threshold, so the finite population correction is correctly not applied here.

B · A point estimate reported as a certainty.

"62% of our shoppers will buy" states a sample statistic as though it were the population parameter. There is no interval, no confidence level, and no acknowledgement that a different 250 shoppers would have given a different number.

p̂ = 155/250 = 0.62 Condition: np̂ = 155 ✓   n(1−p̂) = 95 ✓   both far above 5, so the normal-based interval applies SE = √[0.62 × 0.38 / 250] = √0.00094240 = 0.030699 E = 1.96 × 0.030699 = 0.060169 → ±6.02 percentage points 95% CI = 0.62 ± 0.0602 = [0.5598, 0.6802] → 56.0% to 68.0%

Consequence, in the board's units. Applied to 1.2 million monthly shoppers, the memo's 744,000 buyers becomes a range of 672,000 to 816,000 — a spread of 144,000 shoppers. If the ₱240 million investment breaks even at, say, 700,000 buyers, then the honest answer is that this study cannot tell the board whether the project clears the hurdle. The memo's single figure of 744,000 conceals exactly the uncertainty the board most needs to see, and presenting it without an interval is not a simplification but a material omission.

A second, subtler problem deserves a mark: stated purchase intention is not purchase behaviour. Survey respondents systematically overstate their willingness to buy, and the gap is often large. That is a measurement-validity issue rather than a sampling one — it appears nowhere in the standard error — and it means the 56–68% interval brackets the true stated intention rate, not the true purchase rate.

Audit items C, D and F. For each, state precisely what has been misunderstood, give the correct statement or calculation, and explain the reasoning error in terms a non-statistical board member would follow. For item D, compute the sample size the board's requirement actually needs.

✅ Model answer

C · Two distinct misinterpretations in one sentence.

First error — the probability statement. "There is a 95% probability that the true rate lies inside our confidence interval." The true purchase-intention rate p is an unknown but fixed number, and once the interval [0.5598, 0.6802] has been computed both endpoints are fixed too. The statement "p is between 0.5598 and 0.6802" is therefore either true or false; there is no probability left to assign. What is random is the interval: a different sample of 250 shoppers would have produced a different one. The correct claim is that 95% of the intervals produced by this method contain p, so we are 95% confident — a technical statement about the procedure's reliability.

For the board, in plain language: "A machine that fills bottles correctly 95% of the time has just filled this bottle. The bottle is either right or wrong — the 95% describes the machine. Our interval is the bottle."

Second error — the interval read as a data range. "95% of our shoppers fall within that range" confuses the interval for a proportion with the distribution of individual shoppers. Purchase intention here is a yes/no answer: every individual shopper is at 0 or at 1, and no shopper is at 0.62. The statement is not merely imprecise, it is meaningless for this variable. Even for a continuous variable such as spend, the interval for the mean is narrower than the spread of individuals by a factor of √n — in item A, ₱617 wide against an individual range of roughly ₱269 to ₱2,700.

D · The √n relationship inverted.

The memo makes two errors in one sentence. First, 400 is not double 250 — it is 1.6 times as large. Second, and more importantly, even a genuine doubling would not halve the margin: the margin carries √n in the denominator, so doubling n divides the margin by √2 ≈ 1.414, a reduction of 29%. The actual increase of 1.6× divides the margin by √1.6 = 1.265, a reduction of 21% — which is what the arithmetic below shows.

Current margin at n = 250: ±6.02 percentage points Margin at the proposed n = 400: 1.96√(0.62 × 0.38/400) = ±4.76 pp — an improvement, but far from ±3 The proposal delivers a margin 59% larger than the board asked for. The sample size the requirement actually needs Using the prior estimate p̂ = 0.62: n = 0.62 × 0.38 × (1.96/0.03)² = 0.2356 × 4,268.44 = 1,005.6 → n = 1,006 Using the conservative p = 0.5: n = 0.25 × 4,268.44 = 1,067.1 → n = 1,068 Verify: 1.96√(0.62 × 0.38/1006) = 0.02999 ✓

Which to use? Either is defensible and the difference is only 62 respondents. The prior estimate p̂ = 0.62 comes from a competently executed probability sample of 250, so using it is reasonable and saves about 6% of the fieldwork budget. The conservative p = 0.5 guarantees the margin whatever the truth turns out to be, and is the safer choice if there is any reason to think intention may shift between the two rounds — which, for a product not yet launched, there is. A first-class answer recommends 1,068 and says why the extra 62 respondents are cheap insurance.

Consequence. The unit would have fielded 400 interviews, spent the round-two budget, and delivered ±4.76 points against a requirement of ±3 — discovering the shortfall only after fieldwork closed, when the money was gone and the board meeting was scheduled. The correct n is two and a half times the proposal.

F · Confidence confused with accuracy.

Reporting at 99% instead of 95% does not make an estimate more accurate. It changes the multiplier from 1.960 to 2.576 and widens every interval by about 31% on identical data. The interval is then right more often and says less. Applying it to item B: the 99% interval for purchase intention is 0.62 ± 2.576(0.030699) = [54.1%, 69.9%], against the 95% interval of [56.0%, 68.0%]. In the board's units that is 649,000 to 839,000 buyers instead of 672,000 to 816,000 — a range 31% wider, and correspondingly less able to resolve whether the project clears its hurdle.

For the board, in plain language: "I am 100% confident that between 0 and 1.2 million of our shoppers will buy. That statement is certainly true and completely useless. Every increase in confidence moves us toward that kind of statement." Confidence and precision are traded, not accumulated. The professional default is 95%, and departing from it should be justified in the report — a higher level is appropriate when the cost of being wrong is severe and asymmetric, such as a safety limit or a regulatory threshold, and neither applies here.

A further practical objection: reporting at a non-standard level in a board pack invites the reader to compare these intervals against the 95% figures they see everywhere else, and to conclude that Suki Mart's research is less precise than the competition's. If the level is changed, it must be stated prominently on every exhibit.

✅ Model answer

E · A specification on σ tested by eyeballing s.

"4.1 is close to 3.0, so the supplier is broadly compliant" compares a sample statistic with a contractual parameter by inspection. It is the same error as item B in a different costume: a point estimate treated as though it were the truth. The supplier's promise is about σ, the process standard deviation, and s = 4.1 g is one sample's estimate of it. The right tool is a confidence interval for σ, which requires the chi-square distribution because a sample variance is not normally distributed.

Step 1 · degrees of freedom and the two cut-offs df = 25 − 1 = 24    χ²left = 12.401    χ²right = 39.364 Step 2 · the numerator (n − 1)s² = 24 × 4.1² = 24 × 16.81 = 403.44 Step 3 · the variance interval — larger cut-off under the lower bound lower: 403.44 / 39.364 = 10.249    upper: 403.44 / 12.401 = 32.532 Step 4 · square-root for the standard deviation 95% CI for σ = [3.201 g, 5.704 g]

The finding, stated correctly. The entire interval lies above 3.0 g. The lower bound of 3.201 g exceeds the contractual maximum, so the data give clear evidence that the supplier's process standard deviation is above the promised 3.0 g — and plausibly as high as 5.7 g, nearly double the specification. The memo's conclusion is not merely under-supported, it is the opposite of what the data show. This is the most serious error in the memo, because it is the only one where a correct analysis reverses the recommended action: contractual action is warranted.

Note the asymmetry, which is worth a mark: the interval is not 4.1 ± something. The estimate sits 0.90 g above the lower bound but 1.60 g below the upper, because the chi-square distribution is right-skewed. Note also that the interval is wide — 25 packs pin the mean down well but say relatively little about spread. Doubling to n = 50 would narrow it to roughly [3.43, 5.11]; n = 100 gives about [3.60, 4.76]. Estimating variability well takes considerably more data than estimating a centre, which is worth telling the procurement team before they commission the confirmatory study.

One assumption must be checked and the case states it holds: the chi-square interval requires an approximately normal population, and unlike the t-interval it is not robust to departures. A large n would not rescue it, because the Central Limit Theorem applies to sample means and not to sample variances. Had the weights been skewed, the correct answer would have been to decline the calculation and use a bootstrap interval instead.

The results section as it should have been written.

Category spend. From a random sample of 18 store-months, mean monthly spend per shopper on the target category is estimated at ₱1,485 (95% CI ₱1,177 to ₱1,793; s = ₱620, t-interval with 17 degrees of freedom). The interval is wide because the sample is small: eighteen store-months cannot support a tighter estimate, and revenue projections should be run at both ends of the range rather than at the midpoint.

Purchase intention. From a random sample of 250 shoppers with complete response, 62.0% stated they would buy a Suki Mart private label (95% CI 56.0% to 68.0%, a margin of ±6.0 percentage points). Applied to 1.2 million monthly shoppers this implies 672,000 to 816,000 stated-intent buyers. We note that stated intention systematically exceeds actual purchase, so these figures should be treated as an upper bound on realised demand rather than a forecast of it.

Supplier consistency. From 25 pilot packs, net-weight standard deviation is estimated at 4.1 g (95% CI 3.20 g to 5.70 g, chi-square interval with 24 degrees of freedom; weights approximately normal). The entire interval exceeds the contractual maximum of 3.0 g, so the evidence indicates the supplier is not meeting the agreed variability specification. We recommend a confirmatory study of at least 100 packs and a formal quality review before volume commitments are made.

The recommendation to the board. The defensible position is conditional approval with two gates, and it follows from separating the three findings rather than averaging them.

  • Demand is probably adequate but not established. The intention interval of 672,000–816,000 buyers is encouraging, but it is a stated-intention figure and the range is wide. Before committing ₱240 million, run the round-two survey at n = 1,068 — a cost in the low hundreds of thousands of pesos against ₱240 million, roughly 0.1% of the capital at risk — and, more importantly, commission a small in-store test that measures behaviour rather than intention.
  • The supplier issue is a genuine finding and should gate the launch. A process standard deviation plausibly as high as 5.7 g against a 3.0 g specification is a product-quality and regulatory exposure on a private label carrying Suki Mart's own name. Confirm with n = 100 packs, and make volume commitments contingent on demonstrated compliance.
  • Do not defer indefinitely. Both gates are cheap and fast relative to the decision, and the research unit's errors were errors of estimation rather than of data collection — the underlying samples are sound and can be reused. A realistic timetable is one quarter.

If the board judges it cannot wait, the minimum defensible interim position is to run the financial case at the lower end of the intention interval (672,000 buyers), hold the supplier to a contractual variability clause with a compliance test attached, and record in the minutes which figures were known to be uncertain and by how much at the time of the vote.

Six conclusions and seven failures — one item manages two — and not one of them a data-collection problem — every sample in that memo was competently drawn.
