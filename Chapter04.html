🧠 The one sentence this chapter exists to install

There are three distributions in play, not one — the population, your one sample, and the sampling distribution of the statistic. Almost every error in the next eight chapters comes from confusing the third with one of the first two. If you finish this chapter able to keep them apart, everything after it becomes bookkeeping.

4.1Why we sample at all

Chapter 1 named the four building blocks and warned that the sampling frame is where studies die. This chapter is the promised return to that idea — with the machinery to say how wrong a sample can be, and how much of that wrongness a bigger sample can cure.

A census observes every unit. It has no sampling error at all, and where you have one, you should use it: internal POS data, the payroll file, the full ticket log. But a census is often impossible, and there are four distinct reasons.

💸CostInterviewing 2.4 million mall visitors is not a budget item. Interviewing 1,200 of them is.
⏱️TimeA census that takes nine months answers a question the business stopped asking in month three.
💥DestructivenessTesting every battery to destruction leaves you with no batteries. This is the reason acceptance sampling exists.
🚪AccessibilityYou cannot reach everyone. Some units refuse, some have moved, and some you never knew existed.
🧠 The counter-intuitive part

A well-designed sample is frequently more accurate than a census. A census is a vast operation with vast opportunities for error: rushed enumerators, missed households, inconsistent coding, data-entry fatigue at scale. Concentrating the same budget on 1,200 carefully drawn units, with trained interviewers and follow-up on non-responders, buys quality control that a full enumeration cannot afford. Size is not accuracy. That sentence will come back three more times in this chapter.

🏁 30-second recap

Census where you can; sample where you cannot. Cost, time, destructiveness and accessibility are the four reasons. A good sample can beat a bad census.

4.2The four probability designs

A probability sample is one in which every unit in the population has a known, non-zero chance of selection. That definition is not a technicality — it is the licence that permits every calculation in the rest of this course. Without it there is no standard error, no confidence interval, and no p-value.

Simple random sampling

Every possible sample of size n is equally likely. Operationally: number the frame 1 to N, then draw n numbers at random without replacement. It is the theoretical baseline — every formula in this chapter assumes it unless stated otherwise — and it is often impractical, because it requires a complete numbered list of the population and it may scatter your interviewers across the country.

Systematic sampling

Worked example A frame lists N = 2,400 loyalty members; you need n = 80. Interval k = N / n = 2,400 / 80 = 30 Choose a random start between 1 and 30 — say 17 — then take members 17, 47, 77, 107, …

Systematic sampling is far easier to execute than simple random selection, and in most business settings it performs just as well. Its one genuine danger is periodicity: if k is a multiple of a cycle length in the frame, the sample locks onto one phase of that cycle. Sampling every 7th day of transactions always lands on the same weekday, and so does sampling every 14th or 21st; sampling every 12th unit off a line that packs a dozen per case always takes the same position in the case. The safe rule is to choose a k that shares no common factor greater than 1 with any cycle length in the list — in other words, k and the cycle length should be relatively prime. Against a 7-day cycle, k = 9 is safe (the greatest common divisor of 9 and 7 is 1) and k = 14 is the worst possible choice (their greatest common divisor is 7). A second, quieter danger is a trend in the ordering: if the frame is sorted by value, a systematic sample is well spread but its variance cannot be estimated from the sample itself in the usual way. Before choosing k, ask whether the list has a rhythm or a slope.

Stratified sampling

Divide the population into strata that are internally homogeneous and mutually exclusive, then sample within every stratum. The gain is real: stratification reduces the standard error for the same n, because variation between strata is removed from the error entirely. It also guarantees that small but important subgroups appear.

Proportional allocation, worked A retailer's 2,400 franchisees: Luzon 1,450, Visayas 560, Mindanao 390. Total sample n = 200. Luzon:   nh = 200 × (1,450 / 2,400) = 200 × 0.6042 = 120.83 → 121 Visayas:   nh = 200 × (560 / 2,400) = 200 × 0.2333 = 46.67 → 47 Mindanao: nh = 200 × (390 / 2,400) = 200 × 0.1625 = 32.50 → 32 121 + 47 + 32 = 200 ✓ — 120.83 and 46.67 round normally; 32.50 sits exactly on the boundary and is rounded down so the parts sum to 200. Always check the total, adjust whichever stratum's rounding was closest to the .5 boundary, and state which one you adjusted.
💼 When to over-sample a stratum deliberately

Proportional allocation is the default, not the only option. If Mindanao's 32 franchisees are too few to analyse separately, you may over-sample that stratum — take 60 instead — so that region-level conclusions are possible. The price is that the sample no longer mirrors the population, so every national figure must then be computed with weights that restore the true proportions. Over-sampling without re-weighting is a real and common error: it silently makes the smallest region the loudest voice in the national average, which is precisely the weighted-mean lesson from Chapter 2 arriving in a new setting.

Cluster sampling

Divide the population into clusters — typically geographic — then randomly select whole clusters and study every unit inside them. It exists for one reason: cost. Sending a team to 8 barangays and interviewing everyone is far cheaper than chasing 400 randomly scattered households across a province.

⚠️ Stratified and cluster are opposites, and students reverse them constantly

The memory hook: stratified samples some from every group; cluster samples every unit from some groups. And note the reason cluster sampling costs you precision — units within a cluster tend to resemble one another, so the 400th interview in a barangay tells you much less than a 400th interview drawn from across the province would.

🏁 30-second recap

Probability sampling = known, non-zero chance for every unit; it is what licenses the whole course. SRS is the baseline, systematic is the practical version (watch for periodicity), stratified buys precision, cluster buys cost savings and pays for it in precision. Stratified: some from every group. Cluster: every unit from some groups.

4.3Non-probability designs, and what they cost

In a non-probability sample the chance of selection is unknown, and for some units it is zero. These designs are extremely common in business — most of the "research" you will read in industry uses them — and they are not worthless. What they are is uninferential.

DesignHow units get inLegitimate useThe specific danger
ConvenienceWhoever is easy to reach — mall intercepts, your own customers, students in your classPilot testing a questionnaire; generating hypothesesSystematically excludes the busy, the remote and the uninterested — usually the people whose behaviour differs most
Purposive (judgement)The researcher hand-picks units believed to be typical or informativeExpert interviews; deliberately studying extreme casesThe selection encodes the researcher's prior beliefs, so the study can only confirm them
QuotaFill fixed counts per category — 60 men, 40 women — by any means availableFast commercial polling where speed outweighs rigourLooks like stratified sampling and is not: within each quota, selection is still convenience
SnowballRespondents refer further respondentsHard-to-reach populations — informal traders, specialist professionalsSamples a social network, not a population; isolated members are unreachable by construction
🚫 Quota sampling is the impostor

Quota sampling produces a sample whose margins match the population — the right proportion of each age band, region and sex — and that superficial match is exactly what makes it dangerous. Matching on the variables you thought to control says nothing about the variables you did not. An interviewer filling a "women aged 25–34" quota will still approach whoever looks approachable, so within every cell the selection is convenience. A stratified sample randomises inside each stratum; a quota sample does not. That single difference is what separates a design you may compute a margin of error from and one you may not.

🧠 What is actually lost

Precisely this: the probability in probability sampling is what makes the sampling distribution computable. Without a known selection probability there is no way to say how x̄ would behave across repeated samples — and so:

  • No standard error.
  • No confidence interval, and no "±3 percentage points".
  • No p-value, no hypothesis test, no significance claim.

Any of these attached to a convenience sample is decoration. The software will still print them, which is the whole problem.

💼 How to use a convenience sample honestly

Do not refuse to run one. Run it, and describe it accurately: "We interviewed 240 shoppers at three Metro Manila branches on two weekday afternoons. These respondents are not a probability sample of our customer base, so the figures below describe the respondents and are not generalised to all customers. They are reported to identify themes for a properly designed follow-up." That paragraph costs nothing, protects you completely, and is what separates a professional from someone who was hoping nobody would ask.

🏁 30-second recap

Non-probability designs have unknown selection probabilities, so no standard error, interval or p-value can legitimately be computed. Quota sampling imitates stratified sampling but randomises nothing inside the cells. Use these designs for exploration, and describe them as what they are.

4.4Two kinds of error

This is the distinction that turns a survey report from an advertisement into evidence. Total survey error has two components, and they behave in opposite ways.

Sampling error — the honest one

Sampling error is the difference between your statistic and the parameter, arising purely because you observed some units and not others. It is unavoidable, it is unbiased (across all possible samples the errors average to zero, with no systematic pull in either direction — note that this is a statement about the average error, not a claim that too-high and too-low are equally likely, which is false whenever the sampling distribution is skewed), and — uniquely — it is measurable. The whole apparatus of the standard error exists to put a number on it.

Non-sampling error — the dangerous one

🕳️Coverage errorThe frame omits part of the population. Chapter 1's app-only survey. No amount of data collected from the wrong frame describes the right population.
🚪Non-response errorSelected units do not answer, and the ones who decline differ systematically from the ones who agree.
🗣️Response errorPeople answer inaccurately — leading questions, social desirability, faulty recall, deliberate misreporting of income or spending.
⌨️Processing errorMis-keying, mis-coding, unit mix-ups. Chapter 2's entire subject, arriving after the data is collected.
⚠️ The sentence to memorise

A larger sample buys precision. It never buys validity. Sampling error falls as √n. Coverage error, non-response bias and response error do not fall at all — and a large sample makes them more dangerous, because the tight confidence interval it produces lends false authority to a biased estimate. Precision around the wrong number is worse than imprecision, because it looks convincing.

🧪 Try it — which error is it?

Classify each, then check.

(a) A phone survey reaches only landline numbers. → Coverage — the frame excludes mobile-only households.
(b) Respondents overstate their charitable giving. → Response — social desirability.
(c) Two random samples of 500 give means of ₱3,810 and ₱3,890. → Sampling error — this is the expected, harmless kind.
(d) Only 22% of those contacted reply, and the repliers are mostly retirees. → Non-response.
(e) Revenue in one branch is keyed in pesos while the column is in thousands. → Processing.

Only (c) is measured by the standard error. The other four are prevented, or not at all.

🏁 30-second recap

Sampling error: random, unbiased, measurable, shrinks as √n. Non-sampling error: coverage, non-response, response, processing — systematic, directional, not measurable from the sample itself (a non-response follow-up study is the usual way to size it), and immune to n. Report the response rate and the frame alongside every margin of error.

4.5The sampling distribution

Here is the idea the whole course rests on. Read this section twice.

You draw one sample and compute x̄ = 3,850. That number feels like a fact. It is not — it is one draw from a random process. Had you drawn a different sample you would have got a different x̄. So x̄ is not a fixed quantity at all; it is a random variable with a distribution of its own.

📌 Definition

The sampling distribution of a statistic is the distribution of that statistic's values across all possible samples of size n drawn from the population. It is not a distribution of data. It is a distribution of estimates.

🧠 Three distributions, and you must keep them apart
DistributionWhat varies across itCentreSpread
1 · The populationIndividual unitsμσ
2 · Your one sampleThe n units you happened to observex̄s
3 · The sampling distribution of x̄All possible samples of size nμ (the same μ)σ / √n — the standard error

Row 2 is what you have. Row 1 is what you want. Row 3 is the bridge, and it is the only one of the three you never observe. Every confidence interval and every hypothesis test in Chapters 5 to 12 is a statement about row 3 being used to reason from row 2 to row 1.

Two properties, proved by the figures

μx̄ = μ       σx̄ = σ / √n

The first says the sample mean is an unbiased estimator: across all possible samples it is centred exactly on the truth. It does not say your particular x̄ is correct — it says the procedure does not systematically err in either direction. The second is the standard error, and it is the subject of the next section.

🔬 Verified, not asserted

The distributions in Figures 4.5 and 4.8 were computed by exact convolution of the discrete population — every possible sample outcome enumerated, not simulated. The resulting standard deviation of x̄ matches σ/√n to five decimal places at n = 1, 2, 5 and 30. That is a useful thing to know about a formula you are being asked to trust: it is not an approximation of the spread of sample means, it is the spread of sample means.

🏁 30-second recap

A statistic is a random variable. Its distribution across all possible samples is the sampling distribution. Centre = μ (unbiased); spread = σ/√n. Three distributions: population, your sample, the sampling distribution — never merge them.

4.6The standard error

SE(x̄) = σx̄ = σ / √n

The standard error is the standard deviation of a statistic's sampling distribution. It answers: if I repeated this study, how much would my answer typically move? That is a completely different question from how much do individual units vary?, which is what σ answers.

⚠️ σ versus SE — the error that will cost you the most marks
 Standard deviation, σ or sStandard error, σ/√n

In this chapter's case study an analyst reported "standard error = ₱2,900" when the correct figure was ₱132. The tell was immediate: the resulting interval had a negative lower bound for customer spend.

Sample size for a target precision — invert the formula SE = σ/√n  ⟹  n = (σ / SE)² σ = 6, want SE = 2.0:   n = (6/2)² = 9 σ = 6, want SE = 1.0:   n = (6/1)² = 36 σ = 6, want SE = 0.5:   n = (6/0.5)² = 144
💼 The conversation this formula lets you have

A director asks for "double the accuracy." You can now answer precisely: that means four times the sample, four times the fieldwork budget, and roughly four times the elapsed time. If the budget only supports 1.5× the sample, the honest reply is that the margin of error narrows by about 18%, not 33% — because 1/√1.5 = 0.816. Put the other way, precision (1/SE) improves by a factor of 1.22, not the 1.5 the director is imagining. This is one of the few places where a statistician can convert a vague managerial wish into a costed number on the spot, and it is worth being fluent in.

🏁 30-second recap

SE = σ/√n is the spread of the estimate, not of the data. σ never changes with n; SE always does. Invert to size a study: n = (σ/SE)². Quadruple n to halve the error.

4.7The Central Limit Theorem

Everything so far told you where the sampling distribution is centred and how wide it is. The Central Limit Theorem tells you its shape — and the answer is astonishing enough that it is worth stating carefully.

🔔 The Central Limit Theorem

For a sufficiently large sample size n, the sampling distribution of the sample mean x̄ is approximately normal, with mean μ and standard deviation σ/√n — regardless of the shape of the population distribution, provided that population has a finite mean and a finite variance.

The finite-variance proviso is not a technicality to skip over: σ appears in the conclusion, so a population without one has no σ/√n to converge to. Every business variable you will meet — spend, time, weight, counts — has a finite variance, so the condition is satisfied in practice. It is worth knowing that it exists, because heavy-tailed financial data is the one place where it is genuinely tested.

Read the last clause again. The population may be right-skewed, bimodal, spiky, bounded at zero, or shaped like nothing you have ever seen. It does not matter. Average enough of it and the averages are normal.

🧠 Why this is the most important theorem in the course

Without the CLT, every inferential procedure would need a different formula for every population shape — and you would have to know that shape, which you never do. The CLT collapses all of it onto one curve and one table.

It is also why Chapter 3's normal approximation to the binomial worked. A binomial count is a sum of independent trials, and sums behave like means: as n grows they go normal. That was the CLT in disguise, and you have already used it.

How large is "sufficiently large"?

Population shapen neededNotes
Already normalAny n, even n = 1The sampling distribution is exactly normal, not approximately
Symmetric, not normalSmall — around 10–15Convergence is fast when there is no skew to overcome

⚠️ "n ≥ 30" is a convention, not a law

It is a serviceable default and you should know it. But it is a rule of thumb about moderate skew, and business money variables are frequently worse than moderate. If the population is strongly right-skewed — as revenue, spend and claim amounts almost always are, per Chapter 2 — n = 30 may not be enough, and the honest move is to plot the data and say so. Conversely, if the population is already normal, waiting for 30 is pointless.

The computation, and the contrast that makes it click

🚚 Worked example

Delivery times are normal with μ = 42 minutes and σ = 6. A client's service agreement is breached if the average of a 36-delivery audit exceeds 44 minutes. How likely is that?

Step 1 · the standard error SE = σ/√n = 6/√36 = 6/6 = 1.0 minute
Step 2 · standardise using SE, not σ z = (x̄ − μ) / (σ/√n) = (44 − 42) / 1.0 = 2.00 P(x̄ > 44) = 1 − Φ(2.00) = 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228
🔍 Now do the same 44 minutes for a single delivery
z = (44 − 42) / 6 = 0.3333  →  P(X > 44) = 1 − 0.6306 = 0.3694 A z-table rounded to two decimals gives z = 0.33 and 1 − 0.6293 = 0.3707. The unrounded z = 0.3333 gives 0.3694. Either is acceptable in an exam; the difference is rounding, not method.

One delivery running over 44 minutes happens 37% of the time — utterly routine. The average of 36 running over 44 minutes happens 2.3% of the time — a 1-in-44 event worth investigating. Same threshold, same population, answers differing by a factor of 16. The difference is entirely the divisor, and choosing the wrong one is the most consequential single error available to you in this course.

Two more, for practice P(40 < x̄ < 44) = Φ(2.00) − Φ(−2.00) = 0.9772 − 0.0228 = 0.9545 The middle 95% of possible sample means: 42 ± 1.96(1.0) = [40.04, 43.96] Look hard at that last line. Turn it inside out — around x̄ instead of μ — and it is a 95% confidence interval. That is Chapter 5, and you have just built it.
🏁 30-second recap

CLT: for large enough n, x̄ is approximately normal with mean μ and SD σ/√n, whatever the population's shape. n ≥ 30 is a convention for moderate skew; already-normal populations need no minimum; strongly skewed ones need more. Standardise with SE, never σ. z = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n).

4.8The sampling distribution of a proportion

Half the questions business actually asks are about proportions, not means: what share of customers will renew, what share of units are defective, what share of voters approve. The logic is identical — only the formulas change.

μp̂ = p       SE(p̂) = √( p q / n )     where q = 1 − p

The sample proportion p̂ is unbiased for p, and the normal approximation applies under the same condition you met in Chapter 3:

np ≥ 5  and  nq ≥ 5

Like n ≥ 30, this is a convention rather than a theorem. Bluman uses 5; Agresti and several other texts require 10 or even 15 when the result will be turned into a confidence interval. Use 5 as the working threshold in this course, but treat a borderline case with an extreme p as a reason for caution rather than a pass.

This is the CLT again, wearing different clothes. A proportion is the mean of a variable coded 1 for success and 0 for failure, so everything from §4.7 carries over — including the requirement that n be large enough, which for a proportion depends on how extreme p is.

📱 Worked example

Fifteen per cent of a chain's customers use its loyalty app. A random sample of 200 customers is drawn. What is the probability that more than 20% of the sample turn out to be app users?

Step 1 · check the condition np = 200(0.15) = 30 ≥ 5 ✓     nq = 200(0.85) = 170 ≥ 5 ✓
Step 2 · centre and standard error μp̂ = p = 0.15 SE = √(pq/n) = √[(0.15)(0.85)/200] = √0.0006375 = 0.02525
Step 3 · standardise z = (p̂ − p) / SE = (0.20 − 0.15) / 0.02525 = 1.98 P(p̂ > 0.20) = 1 − Φ(1.98) = 1 − 0.9762 = 0.0238
⚠️ The SE of a proportion uses p, not p̂

The formula √(pq/n) takes the population proportion. In this chapter that is fine — p is given. In Chapter 5, when p is the very thing you are estimating, you will have to substitute p̂ and accept the approximation, and the resulting formula looks almost identical. Keep track of which one a question hands you. A related trap: SE is largest when p = 0.5, so a survey about a genuinely 50-50 issue is the hardest kind to pin down, and a survey about a rare event needs a much larger n to see it at all.

🧪 Try it

Same chain, same p = 0.15, same n = 200. What is the probability that fewer than 10% of the sample are app users?

z = (0.10 − 0.15)/0.02525 = −1.98, so P(p̂ < 0.10) = 0.0238 — identical to the upper tail, because the normal curve is symmetric and 0.10 and 0.20 are equidistant from 0.15.

🏁 30-second recap

p̂ is unbiased for p; SE(p̂) = √(pq/n); the normal approximation needs np ≥ 5 and nq ≥ 5. It is the CLT applied to a 0/1 variable. SE peaks at p = 0.5.

4.9The finite population correction and the Law of Large Numbers

When the population is small enough to matter

Every formula so far quietly assumed that removing a unit from the population does not change what remains — that is, sampling with replacement, or from a population large enough that the difference is negligible. When your sample is a substantial slice of the population, that assumption fails, and it fails in your favour.

SE(x̄) = ( σ / √n ) × √[ (N − n) / (N − 1) ]

The second factor is the finite population correction. It is always ≤ 1, so it always reduces the standard error. The intuition is straightforward: if you have surveyed a third of the population, you already know a great deal about it, and there is correspondingly less left to be uncertain about. In the limiting case n = N you have a census, the factor becomes zero, and the standard error vanishes — as it should.

Worked example — a tenant survey A mall operator has N = 1,240 tenants and surveys n = 400 of them; 68% report satisfaction. n/N = 400/1,240 = 32.3%  →  well above 5%, so the correction is required. SE without correction = √[(0.68)(0.32)/400] = √0.000544 = 0.02332 fpc = √[(1,240 − 400)/(1,240 − 1)] = √(840/1,239) = √0.67797 = 0.82339 SE with correction = 0.02332 × 0.82339 = 0.01920 95% margin: ±1.96(0.01920) = ±3.76 percentage points  — against ±4.57 without the correction
🧠 Omitting the correction is not "being conservative"

Students are often told that ignoring the fpc is the safe choice because it makes intervals wider. That framing is wrong. A wider interval is not safer — it is less informative, and here it discards precision that the organisation already paid for by surveying a third of its tenants. The professional position: apply the correction whenever n/N exceeds 5%, and say that you did. Reporting an unnecessarily wide interval is an error in the same family as reporting an unjustifiably narrow one.

The Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers states that as n increases, the sample mean converges to the population mean. It is the guarantee underneath everything else in this chapter: collect enough data and x̄ will get where it is going.

⚠️ Two things the Law does not say
  • It says nothing about individual outcomes. A roulette wheel that has shown red eight times running is not "due" black — each spin is independent, and the wheel has no memory. The Law works by diluting early results under an ever-growing pile of new ones, not by correcting them. Believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy, and it is the single most expensive misunderstanding in this chapter for anyone who touches trading, insurance or promotions.
  • It says nothing about bias. The Law promises convergence to μ of the population you are actually sampling from. If your frame excludes half your customers, a sample of ten million converges beautifully — on the wrong μ.
🔗 How the two laws divide the work

The Law of Large Numbers says x̄ gets there. The Central Limit Theorem says how fast (σ/√n) and in what shape (normal). The first is a promise; the second is what lets you attach a number to it. You need both, and only the second is usable in a formula.

🏁 30-second recap

Apply the fpc √[(N−n)/(N−1)] whenever n/N > 5%; it reduces the SE, which is a gain not a risk. The LLN guarantees convergence to μ but says nothing about individual outcomes (gambler's fallacy) and nothing about bias. LLN = destination; CLT = speed and shape.

4.10What all of this unlocks

This chapter has been building one object. It is worth stopping to see what it is for.

You know three things about the sampling distribution of x̄: it is centred on μ, it has spread σ/√n, and for large enough n it is normal. Put those together and you can make a statement like this one:

P( μ − 1.96·SE < x̄ < μ + 1.96·SE ) = 0.95

In words: 95% of all possible sample means fall within 1.96 standard errors of the population mean. That is a fact about a distribution you will never observe, and on its own it is useless — you do not know μ.

But the statement can be turned inside out. If x̄ is within 1.96 SE of μ, then μ is within 1.96 SE of x̄. Rearranged:

x̄ − 1.96·SE  <  μ  <  x̄ + 1.96·SE

That is a 95% confidence interval, and you have just derived it. Chapter 5 will spend a session on its interpretation, its variants and its pitfalls, but the machinery is already in your hands — it is nothing more than the sampling distribution read backwards.

🚧 And the standing precondition

Every line above assumes a probability sample from the population you intend to describe. Coverage error, non-response bias and a convenience design do not appear anywhere in these formulas — not because they do not matter, but because the formulas cannot see them. A confidence interval computed on a biased sample is precise, authoritative, and wrong. That is why §4.3 and §4.4 came before §4.7, and why a survey report that states its margin of error but not its response rate should never be trusted.

🏁 30-second recap

Turn "95% of sample means land within 1.96 SE of μ" inside out and you have a confidence interval. Estimation, testing, comparison and modelling are all the same object read in different directions — and all of them presuppose a probability sample.

🚦 The decision cards

🎯 One item or an average?

This one question routes almost every problem in the chapter. One observation → divide by σ. A mean of n observations → divide by σ/√n. The word "average", "mean", or "per branch across the sample" is your signal. Getting this wrong changes the answer by a factor of √n.

📉 Three distributions, never confuse them

Population: spread σ, whatever shape it has. One sample: spread s ≈ σ, same shape. Sampling distribution of x̄: spread σ/√n, normal. Only the third one narrows as n grows, and only the third one is guaranteed a shape.

🚫 The CLT does not fix bias

The CLT is a statement about sampling error under a probability design. It says nothing about coverage error, non-response or a convenience sample. A biased design with n = 50,000 produces a razor-thin interval around the wrong number.

🔢 When does n/N matter?

The fpc is always mathematically valid; the 5% rule decides when it is worth the arithmetic. Compute n/N first. Below 5% → conventionally ignored; the correction is under 2.5%. Above 5% → apply it, and note that it works in your favour: the SE gets smaller, the margin narrower.

📐 The cost of precision

SE falls with √n, so precision is quadratically expensive. Twice as precise = four times the sample. Ten times as precise = one hundred times the sample. Beyond roughly n = 1,000 the curve is nearly flat, which is why national polls stop there.

⚖️ LLN versus CLT

Law of Large Numbers: x̄ converges to μ — the destination. Central Limit Theorem: how fast (σ/√n) and in what shape (normal) — the speed and the map. Only the CLT can be put in a formula.

🏬 Case: Habitat Malls Group — the expansion research report

Background. Habitat Malls Group operates 14 shopping centres across the Philippines, recording roughly 2.4 million customer visits per year and leasing to 1,320 tenant businesses, of which 1,240 are retail and food-and-beverage operators — the tenants for whom mall foot traffic is the relevant commercial driver. The board is deciding whether to commit ₱1.8 billion to a fifteenth mall. A newly formed research unit has produced the report below. All the raw figures are accurate as collected; what you are auditing is how they were gathered and how they were used.

The research unit's report, verbatim:

A. Average spend per visit is ₱3,850. With a standard error of ₱2,900, we are 95% confident the national average lies between −₱1,834 and ₱9,534. Applied to 2.4 million visits, annual shopper spend is therefore ₱9.24 billion.

B. 68% of tenants are satisfied with foot traffic. The standard error is √(0.68 × 0.32 / 400) = 0.02332, so the margin of error is ±4.57 percentage points.

D. Luxury buyers spend ₱18,400 per visit on average, with a 95% interval of ₱16,759 to ₱20,041. We recommend allocating 30% of the new mall's gross leasable area to luxury tenants.

E. The current margin of error on mean spend is about ±₱259. To reach ±₱100 we will double the sample to 960.

F. Because the atrium locations were chosen without any preference for particular shoppers, and because interviewers approached people at random intervals, study A constitutes a random sample of Habitat shoppers nationally.

Of the three studies that actually collected data from a sample — A, B and C — only one rests on a sound sampling design. And not one of the six numbered conclusions in the memo is correct. Your job is to identify the sound design and to find the fault in each of the six conclusions. Be careful with the sound study: its fault is an omission that makes it look weaker than it is, which is easy to read past.

Essay 1 · 20 points · Analyse & Evaluate
Audit the design of studies A, B and C, and evaluate claim F. For each, name the sampling method actually used, classify it as probability or non-probability, identify the specific error family involved, and state the direction in which the resulting estimate is likely to be wrong. Then explain why increasing the sample size would or would not help in each case.
💡 Before you look — what a strong answer must contain
  • A named design for each study, with the feature of the description that identifies it.
  • The distinction between sampling error (shrinks with n) and non-sampling error (does not) applied study by study.
  • A stated direction of bias, not merely the observation that bias exists.
  • Recognition that one of the three designs is sound.
  • A direct refutation of claim F that distinguishes "no interviewer preference" from "known selection probability".
✅ Model answer

A · Shopper spend — convenience sampling, three compounding coverage biases.

Interviewers approached shoppers who "appeared willing" at fixed locations during fixed hours. No shopper has a known, non-zero probability of selection, so this is a non-probability convenience (mall intercept) sample. Three separate exclusions operate at once:

  • Geographic: only the three largest NCR malls, so eleven malls and every region outside NCR are entirely uncovered — yet the conclusion is applied to all 2.4 million national visits.
  • Temporal: Saturday afternoons capture leisure shoppers on the heaviest trading day of the week. Weekday shoppers, evening commuters and quick-errand visitors are absent.
  • Self-selection: shoppers who "appeared willing" and who agreed to stop are those with time to spare — plausibly those on longer, larger shopping trips.

Direction of bias: all three push the same way. NCR malls, Saturday afternoons and unhurried shoppers all have above-average spend, so ₱3,850 is almost certainly an overestimate of the national average. This matters enormously: the ₱9.24 billion revenue figure inherits the bias in full, and it is the number the ₱1.8 billion decision rests on.

Would a larger n help? No. Every additional Saturday-afternoon NCR interview is drawn from the same distorted frame. The estimate would become more precise — a narrower interval around a wrong centre — which is worse than the present situation because a narrow interval reads as authority.

B · Tenant satisfaction — sound design, under-analysed.

A simple random sample of 400 from a complete list of 1,240, with a 100% response rate, is a textbook probability sample. Every retail and F&B tenant has a known selection probability of 400/1240, the frame is complete, and there is no non-response. Note that the population is correctly and narrowly defined — the 80 tenants outside those categories are excluded deliberately, because mall foot traffic is not their commercial driver, and the finding is reported only about the 1,240. This is the one study whose design cannot be faulted — and the point of the case is that it is not the one whose numbers are right (see Essay 2).

C · Renewal intention — probability design defeated by non-response.

The invitation went to a defined list, so the design began as a probability sample. But:

Response rate = 480 / 1,320 = 36.36%  (1,320 = every current tenant, so the frame itself is complete) Missing: 840 tenants, or 63.6% of the intended sample

Nearly two-thirds of the sample is absent, and the achieved sample is now effectively self-selecting. The error family is non-response bias, a non-sampling error. Direction: tenants intending not to renew — those in dispute with the landlord, those winding down, those already committed elsewhere — have the least incentive to complete a landlord's questionnaire. The 71% renewal figure is therefore likely an overestimate, and it is overstating precisely the risk the board most needs to see.

Would a larger n help? Not as proposed, and the proposal makes matters worse in two ways. First, inviting 3,000 contacts at the same 36% rate yields roughly 1,090 responses drawn from the same self-selecting pool: more respondents, identical bias. Second — and more serious — the extra 1,680 contacts are businesses that enquired about leasing and never became tenants. They are not members of the population "current Habitat tenants" at all, so adding them is a coverage error deliberately introduced in the name of a bigger sample. A renewal-intention figure computed over that list would answer no question anyone asked. What would help is raising the rate — reminders, a second mode such as telephone follow-up, incentives — and, critically, a non-response follow-up study: contact a random subsample of the 840 silent tenants by phone and compare their renewal intentions with the 480. If the two groups match, the bias is small; if they diverge, the size of the divergence quantifies the correction needed.

F · The claim of randomness — confuses absence of preference with random selection.

The report's defence is that interviewers had no preference among shoppers. That addresses only interviewer selection bias, and even there imperfectly. Random sampling requires that every member of the target population has a known, non-zero probability of selection. A shopper in Davao on a Tuesday evening had a probability of exactly zero of being interviewed. Zero is a known probability, but the requirement is non-zero, and no procedural fairness at the atrium can repair a frame that never contained most of the population. Note also that "approached at random intervals" is a statement about timing, not about the population — sampling time points randomly within a biased window does not randomise the window.

🚧 The synthesis the board needs

Studies A and C are biased in the same direction — both overstate — and both feed the same decision. The expansion case is built on an inflated spend figure and an inflated renewal rate. Neither error is visible in any standard error, any margin of error, or any confidence interval the report contains.

Then state, for each, what practical consequence the uncorrected figure would have had on the board's decision.

✅ Model answer

A · The standard deviation reported as the standard error.

₱2,900 is s, the spread of individual shoppers. The interval concerns the mean, which requires the standard error:

SE = s/√n = 2900 / √480 = 2900 / 21.9089 = ₱132.37 Margin = 1.96 × 132.3663 = ₱259.44 Correct 95% interval = 3850 ± 259.44 = [₱3,590.56, ₱4,109.44] Report's interval: [−₱1,834, ₱9,534] — width ₱11,368 against the correct ₱518.88 The uncertainty was overstated by a factor of √480 ≈ 21.9.

The negative lower bound is the diagnostic: spend cannot be negative, so an interval for its mean that dips below zero is arithmetically self-refuting. Consequence: as written, the report says the true average could be anything from nothing to ₱9,534 — a range so wide it makes the ₱9.24 billion projection unusable and invites the board to dismiss the research entirely. Correctly computed, the sampling precision is excellent (±₱259 on ₱3,850, about ±6.7%). The irony is exact: the report is far more precise than it claims, and far less accurate, because the real problem is the convenience design of Essay 1, which no formula here can see.

B · The finite population correction omitted.

The uncorrected standard error is right, but the sampling fraction was never checked:

n/N = 400 / 1,240 = 32.26%  →  far above the 5% threshold, so the fpc applies Uncorrected SE = √(0.68 × 0.32 / 400) = √0.000544 = 0.023324 fpc = √[(1240 − 400)/(1240 − 1)] = √(840/1239) = √0.677966 = 0.823387 Corrected SE = 0.023324 × 0.823387 = 0.019205 Correct margin = 1.96 × 0.019205 = ±3.76 pp   (report: ±4.57 pp) Correct interval: 64.2% to 71.8% satisfied

The report overstated its own standard error by 21.4%. Consequence: this is the one error in the whole report that runs against the research unit's interest — it understates the quality of its own best study. Sampling 32% of a finite population genuinely does buy extra certainty, because a third of the uncertainty has been eliminated by observing a third of the population directly. Failing to claim it means a board decision made on a wider interval than the data support, and, in a competitive research market, a unit that looks less capable than it is.

D · The CLT invoked where it does not apply.

n = 12, drawn from a heavily right-skewed population. Two conditions fail at once:

  • The CLT's rough n ≥ 30 guideline already assumes only moderate skew; heavy skew requires substantially more. Twelve is not close.
  • The multiplier 1.96 presumes σ known or n large. With n = 12 and σ unknown, t would be required — and t itself assumes an approximately normal population, which is precisely what a heavily skewed segment is not.

The correct treatment is not to compute a corrected interval but to decline to compute one. Report the 12 observations descriptively — median, range, and the count — and state plainly that the segment is too small for inference. If the luxury estimate matters to the decision (and at 30% of gross leasable area it clearly does), the remedy is a purpose-built study: stratify by buyer segment and allocate a deliberate n of at least 150–200 luxury respondents, recruited through a frame that can actually reach them.

Consequence: this is the most expensive error in the report. A ₱1.8 billion mall would allocate 30% of its leasable area on the strength of twelve self-identified shoppers interviewed on Saturday afternoons in NCR — a sample small enough that two or three unusually wealthy respondents could account for the entire ₱18,400 figure. Note the compounding: study D inherits every bias of study A and adds a fatal sample-size problem of its own.

E · Doubling the sample to halve the margin.

Current margin at n = 480: 1.96 × 2900/√480 = ₱259.44 Proposed n = 960: 1.96 × 2900/√960 = ₱183.45 — not ₱130, and nowhere near ₱100 Required n = (1.96σ/E)² = (1.96 × 2900 / 100)² = (56.84)² = 3,230.6 → n = 3,231 Check: 1.96 × 2900/√3231 = ₱99.99 ✓

Because the margin falls with √n, halving it requires four times the sample and reaching ±₱100 from ±₱259 requires 6.7 times the sample. Consequence: the round-two budget was built on 960 interviews and would deliver ±₱183 — the unit would have spent the money, missed the target, and discovered it only after fieldwork closed. Worth adding for the board: at ±₱100 the marginal value is questionable. If a ±₱259 margin and a ±₱100 margin lead to the same decision, the extra 2,751 interviews buy nothing. And all of it is moot until the convenience design is replaced, because no sample size corrects a biased frame.

🔗 The pattern across the four errors

Three of the four are the same error in different clothes: confusing the variability of individuals with the variability of a statistic. Item A used s where s/√n belonged. Item D forgot that a statistic's distribution needs n to be large before it settles into shape. Item E forgot that the statistic's variability falls with √n rather than n. Item B is the odd one out — an omission rather than a confusion — and it is the only one that made the results look worse than they were.

Its known gap is the cash, non-loyalty visit that ends in no purchase; the honest response is to sample entry events from the traffic counters rather than from purchase records, and to treat zero-spend visits as legitimate observations rather than dropping them — a decision that will lower the mean and should be flagged to the board in advance so the drop is not mistaken for a trading decline.

2 · The design: stratified, on two dimensions. Study A failed on geography, on time and on self-selection. A design that fixes only one of those fixes nothing. Stratify by region and, within each region, by day-type and time block (weekday morning, weekday evening, weekend). Region is the primary stratifier because regional purchasing power differs sharply and because the board needs regional figures in their own right; day-type is the secondary stratifier because it was the second largest source of distortion. The recommended column departs deliberately from proportionality. Under strict proportional allocation Mindanao's 138 respondents would carry a regional margin of roughly 1.96 × 2900/√138 = ±₱484 — too wide to support a regional decision. Raising it to 200 narrows that to ±₱402, and 250 in Visayas gives ±₱359. This is disproportionate allocation for domain precision — not to be confused with Neyman's optimal allocation (nh ∝ Nhσh), which minimises the variance of the national estimate. Neyman would raise Mindanao's share only if Mindanao's spending were unusually variable, which is not the reason for the top-up here — the top-up buys regional precision at a small cost in national precision, and that trade is deliberate. It carries one non-negotiable obligation: because the sample no longer mirrors the population, the national mean must be computed as a weighted average using the true visit shares, not as the simple mean of the 1,500 responses. Forgetting the weights would over-represent Mindanao by a factor of about 1.45 and bias the national figure downward.

5 · Fielding controls. Interviewers approach the shopper selected by the systematic rule, not one who looks approachable; refusals are logged with time and mall and replaced only by the next unit in the systematic sequence; the response rate is reported as a headline figure alongside the mean; and a telephone follow-up of a random 100 refusals tests whether non-respondents differ from respondents.

6 · The three-sentence caveat.

“This estimate is based on a stratified probability sample of 1,500 visits drawn from all 14 malls across four regions and three day-types, with a response rate of [x]%. The margin of error of ±₱[y] at 95% confidence reflects sampling variability only; it does not account for refusals, recall error or any visit outside the counter-log frame. Regional figures are computed within stratum and the national figure is a visit-weighted average, so the two will not agree with a simple average of the regional means.”

7 · The ₱1.8 billion decision. The correct recommendation is neither approval nor abandonment but deferral with a defined cost and timetable. The current evidence base cannot support the decision: the spend figure is biased upward by three compounding exclusions, the renewal figure is biased upward by a 36% response rate, and the luxury allocation rests on twelve observations. A redesigned study of 1,500 visits, a non-response follow-up on the tenant survey, and a purpose-built luxury segment study of 150–200 respondents represent a research cost in the low single-digit millions of pesos against a ₱1.8 billion commitment — on the order of one-tenth of one percent of the capital at risk, for a study that can be delivered within a quarter. Where the board judges that it cannot wait, the minimum defensible interim position is to re-run the projection on a deliberately conservative spend assumption, state that the luxury allocation is unsupported and hold it at a level the mall can re-tenant if the segment does not materialise, and record explicitly in the board minutes which figures were known to be biased and in which direction at the time of the vote.

🎯 What separates a first-class answer

Anyone can say "use a stratified random sample". A first-class answer names the frame and its residual gap, stratifies on the dimensions that actually failed, derives n from a stated precision target, notices that disproportionate allocation forces weighted estimation, and ends with a decision the board can act on this quarter. The statistics are the easy part; connecting them to a ₱1.8 billion commitment is the doctorate-level move.

🏁 Chapter close

Every number in that report was arithmetically traceable, and all six conclusions were wrong. Three were formula errors — the standard deviation used as a standard error, the finite population correction omitted, the sample doubled to halve a margin — and those are the ones a checker catches. The other three were design errors, and no formula in this chapter can see them: a convenience sample described as random, a 36% response rate described as a large sample, and a twelve-person segment described as a market. The arithmetic of sampling is easy; knowing which arithmetic the data has earned is the skill.
