3.1Why probability comes before inference

Chapter 1 gave you the licence to make a claim. Chapter 2 gave you clean data and its shape. Neither gave you a way to say how likely you are to be wrong — and that sentence is the entire product of a statistician.

Recall the promise from Chapter 1: a statistical claim is a promise about people you did not observe, backed by people you did. That promise is only worth something if it comes with a number attached to its uncertainty. "Mean monthly spend is ₱1,325" is a description. "Mean monthly spend is ₱1,325, and we would be surprised if the true figure were outside ₱1,240 to ₱1,410" is inference — and the second half of that sentence is made of probability.

🌉 The bridge, stated plainly

Every inferential technique in this course has the same skeleton:

  1. Assume something about the population.
  2. Work out what samples would probably look like if that assumption were true.
  3. Compare your actual sample against that expectation.

Step 2 is probability. It is the only step that requires new machinery, and it is what this chapter builds. Confidence intervals, p-values, ANOVA, regression diagnostics — every one of them is step 2 wearing a different hat.

Two ways to get a probability

📊Empirical (relative frequency)Count what happened. Of 4,000 transactions last month, 96 were charged back → P(chargeback) ≈ 96/4,000 = 0.024. Most business probabilities are of this kind, and they are only as good as the data behind them.
🎲Classical (theoretical)Reason from equally likely outcomes. A fair die shows a 4 with probability 1/6 — no data collection required. Rare in business, but it is what makes textbook problems solvable by hand.

There is a third kind you will meet in practice and should be able to name: subjective probability — an expert's degree of belief, such as a country manager putting the chance of regulatory approval at 70%. It is legitimate input to a decision model, but it is not measured, and it should never be presented as though it were.

⚠️ The Chapter 2 trap, restated for probability

An empirical probability inherits every defect of the dataset it came from. If your chargeback rate of 0.024 was computed from a table that excluded reversed transactions, the probability is wrong and no amount of correct probability arithmetic downstream will fix it. Clean first, then count, then compute.

🏁 30-second recap

Probability is step 2 of every inferential procedure: what would samples look like if the assumption held? Business probabilities are usually empirical, so they inherit the data's flaws.

3.2Sample space, events, and three axioms

The vocabulary is small and worth getting exactly right, because most probability errors are really vocabulary errors.

TermDefinitionBusiness instance
ExperimentA process whose outcome is uncertain before it happens.Processing one card transaction.
OutcomeOne possible result of the experiment.The transaction is approved.
Sample space, SThe set of all possible outcomes. Must be exhaustive.S = {approved, declined, flagged for review}
EventAny subset of the sample space — one outcome or several.A = "not approved" = {declined, flagged}
Complement, A′Everything in S that is not in A.A′ = {approved}
Mutually exclusiveTwo events that cannot both occur. Their intersection is empty.Approved and declined.
IndependentTwo events where one occurring does not change the other's probability.Two unrelated customers' transactions.

The three axioms

Everything in probability follows from three statements, first set out in this form by Kolmogorov:

  1. Non-negativity. P(A) ≥ 0 for every event A. A probability is never negative.
  2. Normalisation. P(S) = 1. Something in the sample space must happen.
  3. Additivity. If A and B are mutually exclusive, P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B).

Two consequences you will use constantly:

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1      P(A′) = 1 − P(A)
🧠 The complement rule is your shortcut

Whenever a question contains the words "at least one", compute the complement instead. "At least one of 20 units is defective" has 20 messy cases; "no unit is defective" has one. So P(at least one) = 1 − P(none). Students who miss this spend ten minutes on a problem that takes thirty seconds — and it is the most-tested shortcut in the whole chapter.

⚠️ Mutually exclusive ≠ independent

These are constantly confused and they are close to opposites.

Mutually exclusive means they cannot happen together: P(A ∩ B) = 0. Independent means one tells you nothing about the other: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B). Now notice — if A and B are mutually exclusive and both have non-zero probability, then knowing A happened tells you B definitely did not. That is maximum dependence. So two mutually exclusive events with positive probability are never independent.

🏁 30-second recap

Sample space = all outcomes. Event = a subset. Three axioms: non-negative, sums to 1, add for exclusive events. P(A′) = 1 − P(A) — use it for "at least one". Mutually exclusive and independent are different, and mutually exclusive events are never independent.

3.3The addition and multiplication rules

☕ The dataset for sections 3.3 and 3.4

Kapé Manila's loyalty programme has 400 members. 240 use the mobile app; 150 redeemed last month's promotion; 120 did both. Every probability in the next two sections comes from these four numbers.

"Or" means add — then subtract the overlap

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
The addition rule, worked P(App) = 240/400 = 0.600    P(Redeem) = 150/400 = 0.375    P(App ∩ Redeem) = 120/400 = 0.300 P(App ∪ Redeem) = 0.600 + 0.375 − 0.300 = 0.675

"And" means multiply — by the conditional

The general multiplication rule needs no assumptions at all:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B | A)

It simplifies only when the events are independent, because then P(B | A) = P(B):

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)   independent events only
Checking whether app use and redemption are independent P(Redeem) = 0.375     P(Redeem | App) = 120/240 = 0.500 0.500 ≠ 0.375, so the events are not independent — knowing someone uses the app raises the probability they redeemed. Verify with the general rule: P(App) · P(Redeem | App) = 0.600 × 0.500 = 0.300 ✓ The independence shortcut would have given 0.600 × 0.375 = 0.225 — wrong by 25%
🧠 How to test independence in one line

Compare P(B) with P(B | A). If they are equal, the events are independent; if they differ, they are not. Equivalently, check whether P(A ∩ B) equals P(A)·P(B). Do not decide independence by reading the story and forming an impression — compute it. Business events are dependent far more often than students expect, because almost everything in a business is correlated with almost everything else.

With replacement, without replacement

This is where independence is most often lost by accident. Drawing two units from a batch of 50 containing 5 defectives:

Sampling methodP(both defective)Independent?
With replacement
first unit returned before the second draw		(5/50)(5/50) = 0.0100Yes — the batch is identical for the second draw
Without replacement
the realistic case in quality control		(5/50)(4/49) = 0.00816No — removing a defective changes the second probability

The gap here is about 18%, and it grows as the population shrinks. The practical guideline: if your sample is less than about 5% of the population, treating draws as independent is a harmless simplification. Above that, the dependence matters — which is the same 5% threshold that governs the finite population correction you will meet in Chapter 4.

🏁 30-second recap

"Or" → add, minus the overlap. "And" → multiply by the conditional. Drop the conditional only when you have verified independence by comparing P(B) with P(B|A). Sampling without replacement destroys independence, but it is negligible below about 5% of the population.

3.4Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

This is the most commercially important section in the chapter. Every fraud filter, credit scorecard, medical screen, spam classifier and hiring test in existence is a conditional probability — and the reason so many of them are misread is that people reverse the condition without noticing.

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)    read: the probability of A, given that B has occurred

Conditioning does one thing: it shrinks the sample space. When you condition on B, you throw away every outcome in which B did not occur and recompute within what remains. That is why the denominator changes from the grand total to P(B).

Two conditionals from the same 400 members P(Redeem | App) = 120/240 = 0.500   among app users, half redeemed P(App | Redeem) = 120/150 = 0.800   among redeemers, four in five use the app
🚨 Same numerator, different question, different answer

Both fractions have 120 on top. They are 0.500 and 0.800. The only thing that changed was the denominator — which is to say, the group you are talking about. This is the Chapter 2 cross-tabulation lesson in probability notation: the denominator is the question. A marketing manager who reads "80% of redeemers use the app" as "the app makes people redeem 80% of the time" has confused the two, and will over-invest in the app by a wide margin.

Bayes' theorem: reversing the conditional

Often you know P(B | A) but need P(A | B). A test's sensitivity is P(positive | disease); what a patient wants is P(disease | positive). Bayes' theorem performs that reversal:

P(A | B) = [ P(B | A) · P(A) ] / [ P(B | A) · P(A) + P(B | A′) · P(A′) ]

The denominator is just P(B) computed the long way — every route by which B can occur, weighted by how likely each route is. That is why a tree diagram makes the arithmetic almost mechanical.

💳 The worked case: a fraud-detection system

A bank deploys a filter on card transactions. Historically 0.2% of transactions are fraudulent. The vendor's specifications are strong: the filter flags 95% of genuine fraud (its sensitivity), and wrongly flags only 2% of legitimate transactions (its false-positive rate). Operations wants to know: when the filter flags a transaction, how likely is it that the transaction really is fraudulent?

Bayes, worked in full Given: P(F) = 0.002    P(Flag | F) = 0.95    P(Flag | F′) = 0.02    so P(F′) = 0.998 Fraud and flagged:   0.95 × 0.002 = 0.00190 Legitimate and flagged:   0.02 × 0.998 = 0.01996 P(Flag) = 0.00190 + 0.01996 = 0.02186 P(F | Flag) = 0.00190 / 0.02186 = 0.0869, or 8.69%
🧠 The base-rate fallacy, and why it is a business problem

A 95%-sensitive filter produces flags that are wrong 91.3% of the time. Nothing is defective about the filter; the arithmetic is driven by the base rate. When the thing you are hunting is genuinely rare, even a small false-positive rate applied to the enormous non-fraud population generates more false alarms than there are true cases to find.

The managerial consequences are concrete. Freezing 1,996 legitimate customers' cards a month costs you support calls, churn and reputation. And note what the filter is good for: P(fraud | not flagged) = 10/97,814 ≈ 0.01%, so a transaction that passes is almost certainly clean. The correct use is therefore as a screen that clears the bulk safely and hands a manageable queue to human review — not as a decision that automatically blocks a card.

⚠️ Three ways students get Bayes wrong
  1. Answering 95%. That is the sensitivity, P(Flag | F) — the conditional you were given, not the one you were asked for.
  2. Forgetting the base rate. Every Bayes problem has three inputs. Using only the two conditionals is the definition of the base-rate fallacy.
  3. Building an incomplete denominator. P(B) must sum over every route to B. Miss a branch and the answer is too large.
🧪 Try it — change one number

Suppose the bank applies the same filter only to transactions already selected as high-risk, where the fraud base rate is 10% rather than 0.2%. Recompute.

P(Flag) = 0.95(0.10) + 0.02(0.90) = 0.095 + 0.018 = 0.113.   P(F | Flag) = 0.095/0.113 = 0.841.

The identical filter is now right 84% of the time instead of 8.7%. Nothing about the technology changed — only where it was pointed. That is the single most useful practical lesson in this chapter: screening tools should be applied where the base rate is already elevated.

🏁 30-second recap

Conditioning shrinks the sample space; the denominator is the question. P(A|B) ≠ P(B|A). Bayes reverses the conditional, and its denominator sums every route to B. When the base rate is tiny, an accurate test still produces mostly false alarms — so aim screens at populations where the base rate is already high.

3.5Counting rules

Classical probability is favourable outcomes divided by total outcomes, so sometimes you have to count the outcomes. Two rules cover nearly everything, and the whole difficulty is deciding which one applies.

🥇Permutation — order matters
nPr = n! / (n − r)!
Arrangements where position carries meaning: who is President, who is Vice-President.
👥Combination — order does not matter
nCr = n! / [ r!(n − r)! ]
Selections where only membership matters: who is on the committee.
Eight shortlisted candidates, three positions Officers (President, VP, Secretary — distinct roles, so order matters):    8P3 = 8!/5! = 8 × 7 × 6 = 336 ways Committee (three members, no roles — order irrelevant):    8C3 = 8!/(3!5!) = (8 × 7 × 6)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 336/6 = 56 ways The ratio is exactly 3! = 6 — the number of ways to rearrange any one chosen trio.
🧠 The one question that decides it

"If I swap two of the selected items, is it a different result?" If yes, it is a permutation. If no, a combination. And whenever you are unsure, compute the combination and ask whether you need to multiply by r! to account for arrangements — which is exactly the relationship nPr = nCr × r!.

Combinations matter beyond counting problems: nCr is the coefficient in the binomial formula in section 3.7. When you compute the probability of exactly 4 successes in 12 trials, the 12C4 term counts the number of different orders in which those 4 successes could arrive — and because order does not matter for the count of successes, it is a combination.

🏁 30-second recap

Order matters → permutation, nPr = n!/(n−r)!. Order does not → combination, nCr = n!/[r!(n−r)!]. They differ by a factor of r!. nCr reappears immediately as the binomial coefficient.

3.6Random variables and expected value

A random variable assigns a number to each outcome of an experiment. It is the device that turns "the promotion did well" into something you can compute with.

🔢Discrete random variableCountable set of values with gaps. Number of defectives, complaints per day, units sold, calls answered. Described by a probability mass function — a list of P(X = x).
📈Continuous random variableAny value in an interval. Delivery time, revenue, weight. Described by a density function, where probability is area and P(X = any single value) = 0.

A probability distribution for a discrete variable must satisfy two conditions, and checking them is the first thing to do in any exam question:

0 ≤ P(x) ≤ 1 for every x      Σ P(x) = 1

Expected value — the long-run average

E(X) = μ = Σ [ x · P(x) ]

The expected value is a probability-weighted average of the possible values. In a business context it is called expected monetary value (EMV) and it is the standard basis for comparing decisions under uncertainty.

🚀 The worked decision: launch a new product line

Kapé Manila is considering a ready-to-drink bottled line. Marketing puts the outcomes at: strong demand with probability 0.25 and profit ₱8M; moderate demand with probability 0.45 and profit ₱2M; weak demand with probability 0.30 and a loss of ₱3M.

Expected value and risk E(X) = 0.25(8) + 0.45(2) + 0.30(−3) = 2.00 + 0.90 − 0.90 = ₱2.00 million E(X²) = 0.25(64) + 0.45(4) + 0.30(9) = 16.00 + 1.80 + 2.70 = 20.50 Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]² = 20.50 − 4.00 = 16.50 σ = √16.50 = ₱4.06 million
⚠️ An EMV alone is a half-answer

The expected value is ₱2.00M and the standard deviation is ₱4.06M — the risk is roughly twice the expected reward. Two further cautions:

  • ₱2M will never happen. The only possible outcomes are +8, +2 and −3. The expected value is a long-run average over many repetitions, and this launch happens once.
  • A 30% chance of losing ₱3M may be unsurvivable even though the EMV is positive. If the loss would breach a debt covenant, no expected value justifies it. EMV assumes you can absorb the downside and repeat the bet — which is exactly why insurers use it and start-ups cannot.
🧠 The professional habit

Report E(X) and σ together, exactly as Chapter 2 insisted on centre and spread together. It is the same principle in a new setting: a single number describing an uncertain quantity is always incomplete. Then state explicitly whether the organisation can absorb the worst branch of the tree.

🏁 30-second recap

A random variable numbers the outcomes; discrete uses a mass function, continuous a density. Check ΣP(x) = 1 first. E(X) = Σx·P(x) is the probability-weighted average. Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]². Never present an EMV without its standard deviation and a note on survivability.

3.7The binomial distribution

Three named distributions cover most of business, and the binomial is the first. Use it when you are counting how many times something happened out of a fixed number of tries.

🎯 Binomial — the four conditions, all of which must hold
  1. A fixed number of trials, n. Decided before you start, not discovered along the way.
  2. Two outcomes per trial. Success or failure, however you define them.
  3. Constant probability of success, p, on every trial.
  4. Independent trials. One outcome does not change the next.
P(X = x) = nCx · px · qn−x     where q = 1 − p
μ = np     σ² = npq     σ = √(npq)

The formula has three parts and each does a job. pxqn−x is the probability of one specific sequence with x successes. nCx counts how many different sequences produce x successes — which is why section 3.5's combination reappears here. Multiply and you have the total probability of x successes in any order.

📞 Worked example: a sales team's close rate

A Kapé Manila account executive makes 12 corporate pitches this month. Historically 25% of pitches close. Check the conditions: n is fixed at 12 ✓; each pitch closes or does not ✓; the rate is taken as constant at 0.25 ✓; the pitches are to unrelated companies, so independence is defensible ✓.

Centre and spread first μ = np = 12(0.25) = 3.0 closes σ = √(npq) = √[12(0.25)(0.75)] = √2.25 = 1.5 closes
P(exactly 4 closes) P(X = 4) = 12C4 (0.25)4 (0.75)8 12C4 = 12!/(4!·8!) = 495 (0.25)4 = 0.00390625    (0.75)8 = 0.10011292 P(X = 4) = 495 × 0.00390625 × 0.10011292 = 0.1936
P(at least 4 closes) — via the complement P(X ≥ 4) = 1 − P(X ≤ 3) = 1 − [P(0) + P(1) + P(2) + P(3)] = 1 − [0.0317 + 0.1267 + 0.2323 + 0.2581] = 1 − 0.6488 = 0.3512 Four terms instead of nine. This is the complement rule from §3.2 earning its keep.
⚠️ Where the binomial is wrongly applied
  • Sampling without replacement from a small population. Trials are then dependent and p changes. Below 5% of the population it is a harmless approximation; above that it is not.
  • p not actually constant. A close rate that differs by industry, or by which executive is pitching, violates condition 3. Pooling them and calling p = 0.25 hides real structure.
  • n not fixed in advance. "We kept calling until we closed four" is a different distribution entirely — the number of trials is now the random variable.
  • Confusing P(X = 4) with P(X ≥ 4). Read the question. "Exactly", "at least", "at most" and "more than" are four different computations, and "at least 4" includes 4.
🏁 30-second recap

Binomial = successes in a fixed n independent trials with constant p. P(X=x) = nCx p^x q^(n−x); μ = np; σ = √(npq). Shape is set by p: right-skewed below 0.5, symmetric at 0.5. Use the complement for "at least".

3.8The Poisson distribution

The binomial counts successes out of a fixed number of tries. The Poisson counts how many times something happened in an interval — and there is no fixed number of tries, because there was never a defined set of opportunities.

⏱️ Poisson — the conditions
  1. Events occur in a fixed interval of time, area, volume or distance.
  2. A constant average rate, λ, over that interval.
  3. Events occur independently — one arrival does not make the next more or less likely.
  4. Two events cannot occur at exactly the same instant.
P(X = x) = ( e−λ · λx ) / x!     where e ≈ 2.71828
μ = λ     σ² = λ     σ = √λ
🧠 The Poisson's defining oddity

The mean and the variance are the same number. One parameter fixes both centre and spread, which is unlike anything else you will meet. It also gives you a free diagnostic: if your data has a mean of 4 and a variance of 19, it is not Poisson — the events are clustering rather than arriving independently, and condition 3 has failed. Practitioners call that overdispersion, and it is common in business counts because real arrivals bunch (a system outage generates fifty complaints at once).

🚚 Worked example: late deliveries

Kapé Manila's logistics partner averages 4 late deliveries per day across the network. Late deliveries arise from independent causes — traffic, weather, individual vehicle faults — so the Poisson conditions are defensible.

Basic quantities λ = 4    μ = 4    σ² = 4    σ = √4 = 2 late deliveries e−4 = 0.018316
P(a perfect day — no late deliveries) P(X = 0) = (e−4 · 40) / 0! = 0.018316 × 1 / 1 = 0.0183 Fewer than 2 days in 100. Recall 0! = 1.
P(at most 2 late deliveries) P(X ≤ 2) = P(0) + P(1) + P(2) = e−4(40/0! + 41/1! + 42/2!) = e−4(1 + 4 + 8) = 0.018316 × 13 = 0.2381
P(a bad day — 5 or more) P(X ≥ 5) = 1 − P(X ≤ 4) = 1 − [0.01832 + 0.07326 + 0.14653 + 0.19537 + 0.19537] = 1 − 0.62884 = 0.3712 Five decimals are carried deliberately: rounded to four, the five terms sum to 0.6289 and the answer drifts to 0.3711.
⚠️ Scale λ to match the interval in the question

λ is tied to a specific interval. If the rate is 4 per day and you are asked about a half-day, λ becomes 2. Asked about a week, λ becomes 28. Students routinely compute with the given λ regardless of the interval named in the question, and it is one of the most frequent avoidable errors on this topic. Adjust λ first, then compute.

Binomial or Poisson?

AskBinomialPoisson
Is there a fixed number of trials?Yes — n is known: 12 pitches, 20 units, 500 visitorsNo — there is an interval, not a trial count
Can you count the failures?Yes — 12 pitches, 8 failed to closeNo — how many late deliveries didn't happen today?
What is given?n and pλ, a rate per interval
Mean and varianceμ = np, σ² = npq — differentμ = σ² = λ — the same
Typical business useDefect rates, conversion, acceptance sampling, survey responseArrivals, complaints, breakdowns, accidents, claims per period

The sharpest test is the second row: if you cannot count the non-events, it is not binomial — and if the rate is also stable and the arrivals independent, it is Poisson. You can count pitches that failed to close; you cannot count the late deliveries that did not occur. Note the two-step form: ruling the binomial out does not on its own establish the Poisson, which is why the overdispersion check above matters.

🏁 30-second recap

Poisson = events per interval at constant rate λ. P(X=x) = e^−λ λ^x / x!; μ = σ² = λ. Rescale λ when the interval changes. If mean ≉ variance in your data, it is not Poisson. If you cannot count the non-events, it is not binomial.

3.9The normal distribution and the z-transformation

This is the most important distribution in statistics — and not for the reason most people assume. Very little business data is actually normal. What is normal, near enough, is the behaviour of sample means, and that fact is the engine of every inferential procedure from Chapter 5 onward.

🔔 Normal — the properties
  • Continuous and defined for all real values.
  • Symmetric and bell-shaped about μ, so mean = median = mode.
  • Completely specified by two parameters: μ locates it, σ sets its width.
  • Total area = 1. Probability is area under the curve.
  • The tails approach zero but never touch it — no value is strictly impossible.
⚠️ For a continuous variable, P(X = any single value) = 0

There is no area above a single point, so the probability that a delivery takes exactly 42.000… minutes is zero. This is not a technicality with no consequences — it means P(X ≤ 42) = P(X < 42), so you never have to fuss about whether an endpoint is included. That convenience disappears for discrete variables, which is precisely why §3.10 needs a continuity correction.

The standard normal, and why one table suffices

There are infinitely many normal distributions — one for every pair (μ, σ). Tabulating them all is impossible. The z-transformation converts any of them into the single standard normal distribution with μ = 0 and σ = 1:

z = (x − μ) / σ

A z-score is the same quantity you met in Chapter 2 — distance from the mean measured in standard deviations. What is new here is its use: once a value is expressed as a z, a single table gives you the probability.

🚚 Worked example: quoting a delivery window

Kapé Manila's delivery times are approximately normal with μ = 42 minutes and σ = 6 minutes. Operations needs three answers: how often deliveries beat 36 minutes; how often they land between 36 and 54; and what window it can advertise while meeting it 95% of the time.

1 · P(delivery under 36 minutes) z = (36 − 42) / 6 = −1.00 From the table, Φ(−1.00) = 0.1587 — about 16% of deliveries
2 · P(between 36 and 54 minutes) z1 = (36 − 42)/6 = −1.00     z2 = (54 − 42)/6 = +2.00 P(−1 < z < 2) = Φ(2.00) − Φ(−1.00) = 0.9772 − 0.1587 = 0.8185 Always subtract the smaller cumulative area from the larger. Using unrounded values gives 0.8186; the 0.0001 gap is table rounding.
3 · The 95% delivery window — working backwards We need the x below which 95% of deliveries fall, so find z with Φ(z) = 0.9500. The table gives 0.9495 at z = 1.64 and 0.9505 at z = 1.65, so by convention z = 1.645. Rearranging z = (x − μ)/σ gives x = μ + zσ x = 42 + 1.645(6) = 42 + 9.87 = 51.87 minutes Advertise a 52-minute window and you will meet it about 95% of the time.
🧠 Two directions, one formula

Forward — you have a value, you want a probability: compute z, look up the area. Backward — you have a probability, you want a value: look up z for that area, then x = μ + zσ. Nearly every normal-distribution exam question is one of these two, and the first thing to do is decide which. Sketching the curve and shading the region you want takes ten seconds and prevents most sign errors.

🏁 30-second recap

Normal = continuous, symmetric, fixed by μ and σ; probability is area; P(X = a point) = 0. z = (x − μ)/σ collapses every normal onto one table. Forward: value → z → area. Backward: area → z → x = μ + zσ. The empirical rule is just three central areas of this curve.

3.10The normal approximation to the binomial

Computing P(X ≥ 40) for a binomial with n = 200 means summing 161 terms. Before software that was impractical, and even with software the approximation is worth understanding — because it is the first appearance of the idea that makes all of inferential statistics work.

🌉 What is really going on here

A binomial count is a sum of n independent trials. As n grows, that sum becomes approximately normal — regardless of p. This is a special case of the Central Limit Theorem, the result Chapter 4 is built on. So this section is not a computational trick from the era before computers; it is a preview of the single most important theorem in the course.

When you may use it

np ≥ 5   and   nq ≥ 5    some texts require 10; check which your instructor uses

Both conditions matter. If p is very small, np can fail even for large n — with n = 200 and p = 0.01, np = 2, and the binomial is still badly skewed. In that situation the Poisson is the better approximation, not the normal.

When the conditions hold, match the normal's parameters to the binomial's:

μ = np      σ = √(npq)

The continuity correction — the step everyone forgets

You are replacing a discrete variable, which lives on whole numbers, with a continuous one, which spreads across an interval. A binomial bar at x = 40 occupies the interval from 39.5 to 40.5 under the continuous curve. Ignore that half-unit and you systematically cut off part of the area you wanted.

You wantUseReasoning
P(X = 40)P(39.5 < X < 40.5)The whole bar
P(X ≥ 40)P(X > 39.5)Include all of the bar at 40
P(X > 40)P(X > 40.5)Exclude the bar at 40
P(X ≤ 40)P(X < 40.5)Include all of the bar at 40
P(X < 40)P(X < 39.5)Exclude the bar at 40

The rule in words: move the boundary outward by 0.5 when the value is included, inward by 0.5 when it is excluded. Sketching two or three bars around the boundary makes the direction obvious every time.

📱 Worked example: app adoption

Of Kapé Manila's customers, 15% use the loyalty app. In a random sample of 200 customers, what is the probability that at least 40 are app users?

Step 1 · check the conditions np = 200(0.15) = 30 ≥ 5 ✓     nq = 200(0.85) = 170 ≥ 5 ✓
Step 2 · match the parameters μ = np = 30     σ = √(npq) = √[200(0.15)(0.85)] = √25.5 = 5.0498
Step 3 · apply the continuity correction and convert P(X ≥ 40) becomes P(X > 39.5) z = (39.5 − 30) / 5.0498 = 9.5 / 5.0498 = 1.88 P(z > 1.88) = 1 − Φ(1.88) = 1 − 0.9699 = 0.0301
⚠️ The approximation is an approximation

Even done correctly it returns 0.0301 against an exact 0.0335 — close enough for a staffing decision, not close enough for a compliance threshold. Two habits follow. First, when the exact binomial is computable, compute it; the approximation exists for hand calculation and for insight, not because it is better. Second, the approximation is worst in the far tails and when p is far from 0.5, which is exactly where regulatory and risk questions tend to live.

🚦 The decision cards

🎯 Which distribution?

Measuring a continuous quantity → normal. Counting successes in a fixed n with countable failures → binomial. Counting events per interval with no countable non-events → Poisson.

🔄 Bayes in one line

You have P(B|A) and need P(A|B). Multiply along each tree branch, sum the branches that end in B, and take the A-branch's share. Three inputs: two conditionals and the base rate.

🚫 Exclusive ≠ independent

Mutually exclusive: P(A ∩ B) = 0. Independent: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B). Two mutually exclusive events with positive probability are never independent.

± The continuity correction

Value included → move the boundary outward 0.5. Value excluded → move inward 0.5. So P(X ≥ 40) → P(X > 39.5) but P(X > 40) → P(X > 40.5).

📐 Normal, two directions

Forward: value → z = (x−μ)/σ → area from the table. Backward: area → z from the table → x = μ + zσ. Sketch and shade first.

🔢 z-values to memorise

1.645 → 5% in one tail · 1.96 → 5% split across two tails · 2.33 → 1% one tail · 2.58 → 1% two tails.

🧾 Key terms glossary

Sample space (S)The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. Must be exhaustive; P(S) = 1.
EventAny subset of the sample space — one outcome or a collection of them.
Complement (A′)Everything in S that is not in A. P(A′) = 1 − P(A).
Mutually exclusiveTwo events that cannot both occur; P(A ∩ B) = 0. Never independent if both have positive probability.
Independent eventsEvents where one occurring does not change the other's probability: P(B | A) = P(B).
Conditional probabilityP(A | B) — the probability of A within the reduced sample space in which B has occurred.
Bayes' theoremThe rule for reversing a conditional, combining a base rate with two conditional probabilities.
Base rateThe unconditional prevalence of an event, P(A). Omitting it from a Bayes calculation is the base-rate fallacy.
SensitivityP(positive test | condition present) — the share of true cases a screen detects.
False-positive rateP(positive test | condition absent) — the share of clean cases a screen wrongly flags.
PermutationAn ordered arrangement of r items from n. nPr = n!/(n−r)!.
CombinationAn unordered selection of r items from n. nCr = n!/[r!(n−r)!]. Reappears as the binomial coefficient.
Random variableA rule assigning a number to each outcome of an experiment. Discrete or continuous.
Probability distributionA complete statement of the values a random variable can take and their probabilities. ΣP(x) = 1.
Expected valueE(X) = Σx·P(x) — the probability-weighted average, and a long-run average rather than a forecast for one trial.
Expected monetary valueAn expected value whose outcomes are payoffs. Assumes the decision-maker can absorb the downside and repeat the bet.
Binomial distributionThe number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials with constant p.
Poisson distributionThe number of events in a fixed interval at a constant average rate λ, with mean equal to variance.
OverdispersionCount data whose variance exceeds its mean, indicating clustered rather than independent arrivals — so not Poisson.
Normal distributionA continuous, symmetric, bell-shaped distribution specified by μ and σ, in which probability is area under the curve.
Standard normalThe normal distribution with μ = 0 and σ = 1, to which all others are converted by the z-transformation.
Continuity correctionThe ±0.5 adjustment applied when a discrete distribution is approximated by a continuous one.
Central Limit TheoremThe result that sums and means of independent observations tend toward normality as n grows. Previewed by the normal approximation; developed in Chapter 4.

🛵 Case: Padala Express — the quarterly operations memo

Background. Padala Express runs last-mile parcel delivery across Metro Manila using a pool of contracted motorcycle riders. A newly hired operations analyst has prepared a memo covering four operational questions and one strategic one. The underlying figures below are all correct; the analyst's use of them is what you are auditing.

#QuantityWhat the historical data saysQuestion management asked
ARider no-shows40 riders are scheduled per shift; 8% independently fail to log inHow likely is a shift with 8 or more no-shows? (8 is the point at which service degrades.)
BParcel damage claimsAn average of 2.5 claims per day, arising from independent causesWhat is the probability of a completely claim-free week?

A. With 40 riders and an 8% no-show rate, no-shows are normally distributed with μ = 3.2 and σ = 1.72. So P(X ≥ 8) = P(z > 2.80) = 0.0026. A degraded shift is a once-in-400-shifts event and needs no contingency planning.

B. Claims follow a Poisson distribution with λ = 2.5. The probability of a claim-free week is therefore e−2.5 = 0.082, or about one week in twelve.

D. The screening model is 88% accurate, so when it flags a rider we can be 88% confident the rider is genuinely high-risk. I recommend automatically deactivating every flagged rider.

E. The Cebu expansion has an expected monetary value of +₱1.3M. Since the EMV is positive, we should proceed.

F. Separately, note that rider no-shows and heavy rainfall are independent events, so the probability of both occurring on a given day is simply P(no-show) × P(rain).

✅ Model answer

A · Rider no-shows — wrong distribution.

The structure is binomial: a fixed n = 40, two outcomes per rider, a constant p = 0.08, and independence stated. So the exact binomial is the correct model. The analyst instead used the normal approximation, and the validity check disqualifies it:

np = 40(0.08) = 3.2   →   fails the np ≥ 5 condition nq = 40(0.92) = 36.8 ✓    but both conditions must hold

With np = 3.2 the binomial is still strongly right-skewed, and a symmetric curve cannot represent it. The analyst also omitted the continuity correction, compounding the error. Correct answers:

Exact binomial: P(X ≥ 8) = 0.0127 Poisson approximation, λ = np = 3.2: P(X ≥ 8) = 0.0168 — the right approximation for large n and small p Analyst's figure: 0.0026 The analyst understated the true probability by a factor of about 4.9.

Consequence: a degraded shift is not a once-in-400 event but roughly once in 79 shifts — around five times a year on a daily-shift schedule. The memo's conclusion that no contingency planning is needed is exactly inverted. This is also the more dangerous direction of error: the approximation failed in the tail, and it failed by understating risk.

B · Damage claims — right distribution, wrong λ.

Poisson is correct: there is an interval, a constant average rate, independent causes, and no countable set of claims that failed to occur. But λ is tied to an interval, and the question asks about a week:

λweek = 2.5 × 7 = 17.5 P(claim-free week) = e−17.5 = 2.5 × 10−8 Analyst's figure (using the daily λ): 0.082 Overstated by a factor of roughly 3.3 million.

Consequence: the analyst has promised management a claim-free week about once a quarter. The true figure means it would occur roughly once every 760,000 years. Any claims-reserving or staffing assumption built on "one clean week in twelve" is worthless. Note that 0.082 is a real quantity — the probability of a claim-free day — which is what makes this error so easy to make and so easy to miss.

C · Delivery window — correct.

The variable is continuous and stated to be approximately normal, so the normal is right, and this is a backward problem: we need the value below which 90% of deliveries fall. Φ(z) = 0.9000 gives z ≈ 1.28, and x = μ + zσ = 38 + 1.28(7) = 46.96, so a 47-minute window. The analyst's method, z-value and arithmetic are all sound. Two refinements a strong answer would add: the estimate depends on the normality assumption, which should be checked with a histogram or a normal probability plot before it is advertised; and rounding up to 47 is the conservative direction, which is the right way to round when the number becomes a public commitment.

F · Independence claim — indefensible.

Rider no-shows and heavy rainfall are almost certainly dependent: motorcycle riders are far less likely to log in during a downpour. The multiplication shortcut P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B) requires independence, and the correct general rule is P(A ∩ B) = P(rain) × P(no-show | rain). Because P(no-show | rain) will exceed P(no-show), the shortcut understates the joint probability — and it understates it precisely on the days when a staffing shortfall matters most. The test is empirical, not intuitive: compare the observed no-show rate on rainy days with the overall rate. If they differ, the events are dependent, and the memo should say so.

✅ Model answer

What "88%" is. It is the model's sensitivity: P(Flag | Risky). Management asked for the reverse, P(Risky | Flag). These are different quantities, and the analyst has answered the question the vendor's brochure answers rather than the question the business asked.

Bayes, in full P(Risky) = 0.03    P(Flag | Risky) = 0.88    P(Flag | Not risky) = 0.06    P(Not risky) = 0.97 Risky and flagged: 0.88 × 0.03 = 0.0264 Not risky and flagged: 0.06 × 0.97 = 0.0582 P(Flag) = 0.0264 + 0.0582 = 0.0846 P(Risky | Flag) = 0.0264 / 0.0846 = 0.3121, or 31.2%

The same result in riders, which is how to present it.

Of 846 flags, 264 are genuine — 31.2%. So 582 of every 846 riders deactivated would have been safe, and roughly two riders lose their livelihood for every one genuine risk removed.

Why, in plain language. The model is good at what it was measured on: it catches 264 of the 300 genuinely risky riders. The problem is the arithmetic of the population it is applied to. Only 3% of riders are risky, so there are 9,700 safe riders — and even a small 6% error rate on that large group produces 582 false alarms. 582 swamps 264 because the clean population is 32 times larger than the risky one. The model is not defective; the base rate is doing the damage.

Where the model is genuinely valuable. Look at the other conditional: P(Risky | Not flagged) = 36 / 9,154 = 0.39%, against a background rate of 3%. A rider who passes the screen is roughly eight times safer than an unscreened rider. That is real, usable information — the model is an effective tool for clearing the bulk of the pool, and a weak tool for condemning individuals.

Recommended deployment.

  1. Do not auto-deactivate. A 31% precision rate cannot support an irreversible action against a person's income, and the 582 wrongly deactivated riders would generate churn, recruitment cost and plausible legal exposure.
  2. Use the flag to prioritise review, not to decide. 846 flags per 10,000 riders is a tractable queue for a safety team, and human review adds independent information the model does not have.
  3. Apply proportionate interventions instead of removal — a refresher module, a ride-along assessment, closer monitoring for a defined period. These are reversible, so a false positive costs little.
  4. Target the model where the base rate is already elevated. Riders with a recent incident, a customer complaint or a licence issue form a segment with a higher prior. At a 15% base rate the same model reaches 72% precision; at 30% it reaches 86%. Nothing about the technology changes — only where it is pointed.
  5. Monitor the realised precision. Track what proportion of flagged riders subsequently cause an incident, and recalibrate; the 3% base rate is itself an estimate and it will drift.

What would justify automatic deactivation. Three changes, and they are not equally easy. (i) A far lower false-positive rate — dropping it from 6% to 0.5% at the same base rate lifts precision to about 84%. (ii) Deployment restricted to a high-base-rate segment, as above. (iii) A change in what "deactivation" means: if it were a reversible 48-hour suspension pending review rather than permanent removal, the cost of a false positive falls far enough that a 31% precision rate might be tolerable. In practice (ii) and (iii) are achievable now; (i) requires a better model.

Evaluating the analyst's recommendation. The EMV is positive, and the analyst's arithmetic is correct — but "positive EMV, therefore proceed" is the error. Three points dismantle it:

  1. ₱1.3M will never happen. The only possible outcomes are +15, +4 and −20. The expected value is a long-run average across many repetitions of this gamble, and the company launches in Cebu once.
  2. The downside is fatal, not merely bad. A ₱20M loss against ₱16M of equity is a ₱4M shortfall — insolvency — and it carries a 25% probability. One chance in four of ceasing to exist is not a risk that a ₱1.3M expected gain compensates.
  3. EMV assumes you survive to repeat the bet. That is the unstated premise, and it is exactly why insurers can rely on expected value while a single-shot decision-maker cannot. A firm that is wiped out on the first draw never reaches the long run in which the average materialises.

The recommendation I would send.

Do not approve the expansion as structured. The proposal is not unattractive — a positive expected value and a 75% chance of a gain are genuinely encouraging — but in its current form a single unfavourable outcome ends the company. The objective is therefore to keep the upside while removing the fatal branch. Four routes, in the order I would pursue them:

  1. Stage the entry. Commit only enough capital to test the Cebu market — two or three barangays rather than the full city — with a defined go/no-go review. This converts one ₱20M bet into a small bet followed by an informed larger one, and the information gained changes the probabilities for the second decision.
  2. Cap the downside contractually. Lease rather than buy fleet and depot capacity, negotiate break clauses, and use revenue-share rather than fixed-fee rider contracts. If the worst branch can be capped at, say, ₱8M, the decision becomes survivable and the EMV argument regains its force.
  3. Share the risk. A joint venture with a Cebu-based logistics partner halves both the upside and the downside. Halving a ₱20M loss to ₱10M brings it inside the equity base.
  4. Interrogate the 0.25. Where did the weak-demand probability come from? If it is a subjective management estimate rather than a measured figure — which the case does not tell us — then the entire calculation rests on a judgement, and it is worth spending on market research before spending on trucks. A sensitivity analysis would show how much the recommendation changes if the weak-demand probability were 0.15 or 0.35.

What I would say about the memo itself. Item E is not a computational error — the EMV is right. It is a reporting failure: a single number describing an uncertain quantity was presented without its spread and without a check on whether the organisation could absorb the worst outcome. That is the same discipline Chapter 2 demanded of the mean, arriving in a new setting. Never present an expected value alone.

Every figure in that memo was arithmetically defensible on its own terms, and four of the five conclusions were wrong.
