Hypothesis Testing:
One-Sample Tests

Chapter 5 asked "what is μ, roughly?" This chapter asks a sharper question: "could μ plausibly be this?" It is the same machinery pointed at a different target — and it is the single most misused procedure in business, science and journalism.

⏱️ ~70 min read 🧮 10 worked examples 📊 12 figures 🎮 40 quiz items 🎯 CLO6
⚡ TL;DR — the whole chapter in 7 lines
  1. A test is an argument by contradiction: assume nothing has changed, work out what the data should then look like, and see whether what you got is hard to live with.
  2. H₀ always carries the equals sign. H₁ carries the claim you are trying to establish, and it decides which tail you look in.
  3. Every test statistic has the same shape: (estimate − claimed value) ÷ standard error. Four situations, four denominators, one idea.
  4. The p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if H₀ were true. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, and it is not a measure of effect size.
  5. Two ways to be wrong: a false alarm (Type I, probability α, chosen by you) and a missed effect (Type II, probability β, mostly a consequence of your sample size).
  6. Failing to reject H₀ is not proving it. An underpowered study fails to reject almost everything, and reporting that as "no difference" is the most common error in this chapter.
  7. Statistical significance is not business importance. With enough data any difference becomes significant — always report the effect size and the interval too.

🧠 The one sentence this chapter exists to install

A p-value tells you how surprising your data would be if nothing were going on — and nothing else. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, it is not the probability that you have made a mistake, and it says nothing whatever about whether the effect is big enough to act on. Almost every abuse of statistics you will meet in a boardroom, a newspaper or a journal is a version of forgetting that sentence.

6.1The logic of a test

Chapter 5 ended with a comparison you made four times without naming it. The franchise target of 33% fell inside the food-cost interval, so you declined to claim a difference. The supplier's 3.0 g specification fell outside the interval for σ, so you concluded the supplier was failing. That reasoning has a name, a vocabulary and a formal procedure, and this chapter is all three.

Why you cannot simply prove the thing you want

Suppose a BPO's service-level agreement caps average handle time at 330 seconds, and a sample of 100 calls comes back at 312 seconds. Management wants to claim the process is comfortably under the cap. How would you prove that?

You cannot. Proving "μ is below 330" would require checking every call, which is the whole reason you sampled in the first place. What you can do is far more useful, and it is the oldest trick in mathematics: assume the opposite, and show that the assumption leads somewhere absurd.

⚖️ The courtroom analogy, used carefully

A criminal trial begins by assuming innocence — not because the court believes it, but because that is the assumption the evidence must overturn. The prosecution then presents data. If the evidence would be very unlikely for an innocent person, the jury convicts. If not, the verdict is "not guilty", which is emphatically not "proven innocent".

The analogy carries almost all the way, and the one place it strains is worth knowing: a jury is asked to weigh the probability of guilt, whereas a statistical test only ever computes the probability of the evidence under the assumption of innocence. That distinction is exactly the misreading §6.5 is about — but for now, the shape is right, and the crucial half of it is the verdict: failing to convict is not an acquittal on the merits.

🚫 The word that is never used

You will not find the phrase "accept the null hypothesis" anywhere in this chapter, and you should not write it in an exam. A test that fails to reject H₀ has established only that the data are compatible with H₀ — and they are usually compatible with a great many other values too. The food-cost interval [31.61%, 33.39%] is compatible with 33.0%, but equally with 32.0% and 33.2%. Failing to reject 33.0% does not single it out; it merely fails to rule it out, along with everything else in the interval.

The same machinery, pointed differently

It is worth being explicit about what has and has not changed since Chapter 5. The sampling distribution is the same object. The standard error is computed the same way. The z and t tables are the same tables. What changes is the question:

📏 Estimation asks

"What values of μ are plausible, given this sample?" The answer is a range, and it is the right tool when nobody has yet put a specific number on the table.

⚖️ Testing asks

"Is this particular value of μ plausible, given this sample?" The answer is a verdict, and it is the right tool when a contract, a target, a specification or a competitor's claim has already named a number.

Section 6.9 will show that for a two-sided test these two questions are answered by the same arithmetic, so they can never disagree. Knowing that in advance should make the rest of the chapter feel less like new material and more like a change of emphasis.

🏁 30-second recap

A test assumes H₀ (the dull claim, always with an equals sign), derives what the data should look like under it, measures the gap with a test statistic, and rejects H₀ only if that gap would be unlikely. It never proves H₀ and it never proves H₁ — and the phrase "accept the null" does not exist.

6.2Stating the hypotheses, and choosing a tail

More marks are lost here than anywhere else in the chapter, and more real-world tests are invalidated here than by any arithmetic error. Getting the hypotheses right is not a formality — it determines the entire calculation that follows.

Three rules that settle almost every case

1️⃣ H₀ gets the equals sign

Always. H₀ is μ = 330, or p = 0.12, or σ = 3.0. The test computes a sampling distribution, and it needs one specific value to build it around. Since the SLA example is left-tailed (H₁: μ < 330), its compound form would be μ ≥ 330 — and that does not name a single centre, so a test of it is always evaluated at the boundary, μ = 330. That is the value inside H₀ least favourable to rejection: data surprising under 330 are even more surprising under any larger μ. Many textbooks write the compound form, and it gives identical arithmetic; writing H₀: μ = 330 simply makes that mechanism visible.

2️⃣ H₁ carries the claim

Whatever the researcher is trying to establish goes in H₁. If a manager wants evidence that handle time has fallen, H₁ is μ < 330. The burden of proof sits on H₁, which is why a test can support it but never support H₀.

3️⃣ The tail follows H₁

H₁ with < → left tail. With > → right tail. With ≠ → both tails, α split between them. The direction is decided by the wording of H₁ and by nothing else.

🧭 The question that resolves the tail

"What would I need to see in order to act?" If only an increase would change your decision, use a right tail. If only a decrease would, use a left tail. If a departure in either direction matters — as with a target you are supposed to hit rather than beat — use two tails. Notice that the food-cost example is genuinely two-tailed: running far below target is as much a problem as running above it, because it usually means portion sizes or quality have been cut.

The one-tailed temptation, and why it is cheating

A one-tailed test is more powerful in the direction you chose. Its critical value is 1.645 rather than 1.960, so a borderline result that fails a two-tailed test may pass a one-tailed one. That creates an obvious temptation, and it has a name.

🚨 Post-hoc tail selection

Consider a campaign test with H₀: p = 0.12, n = 1,600, and 216 conversions, so p̂ = 0.135. The two-tailed p-value is 0.0648 — not significant at α = 0.05. Switching to a one-tailed test after seeing that the result went the "right" way halves it to 0.0324, and the finding is suddenly reportable.

This is invalid, and the reason is precise: by choosing the tail after seeing the direction, you have effectively kept both tails available, so your real Type I error rate is 10%, not 5%. You have doubled your false-alarm rate and reported it as though you had not. The tail must be fixed before the data are seen, and in a report it should be justified from the business question rather than from the result. Where the direction genuinely could not have been anticipated, the honest choice is two tails.

✍️ Reflect

A quality manager writes: "H₀: the process is fine. H₁: the process is broken." Both parts are wrong as stated — neither names a parameter or a value, and there is nothing to build a sampling distribution around. Rewrite them for a filling line whose target net weight is 250 g. Then ask yourself the harder question: should this be one-tailed or two? Underfilling cheats the customer and attracts regulatory attention; overfilling costs money on every unit. What does that imply?

🏁 30-second recap

H₀ always carries the equals sign; H₁ carries the claim you want to establish; the tail follows H₁'s direction. Two-tailed tests split α, so their cut-offs sit further out (±1.960 against 1.645). Choosing the tail after seeing the data doubles your true Type I error rate and invalidates the test.

6.3The two ways to be wrong

A test produces a verdict from incomplete information, so it will sometimes be wrong. There are exactly two ways, they are not symmetric, and choosing how to balance them is a business decision that statistics cannot make for you.

🚨 Type I error — the false alarm

Rejecting H₀ when it is actually true. You announce a change that did not happen.

Its probability is α, and it is chosen by you before the test. Set α = 0.05 and you are accepting that, in a world where nothing is going on, you will raise a false alarm about one time in twenty.

🕳️ Type II error — the missed effect

Failing to reject H₀ when it is actually false. A real change happens and your study does not notice.

Its probability is β, and it is not chosen — it is a consequence of α, the sample size, the variability of the data, and how big the real effect is. 1 − β is called the power of the test.

Why they trade against each other

The decision rule is a line drawn on the sampling distribution. Move it in either direction and one error rate falls while the other rises — there is no position that reduces both.

📐 The relationship, precisely

Note two things the figure makes visible and that words alone do not.

  • β is not a single number. It depends on which alternative is true. Against μ = 320, β = 0.282. Against μ = 325 it is 0.703; against μ = 312 it is 0.009. A test that easily catches a large effect may be nearly blind to a small one, so "the" Type II error rate is meaningless without naming an effect size.
  • α + β does not equal 1. They are computed under different assumptions — α under H₀, β under a specific H₁ — so they are not complements and there is no arithmetic linking them beyond the shared decision line.

Choosing α is a business judgement, not a convention

The habit of using α = 0.05 is exactly that — a habit, dating to Fisher's tables in the 1920s. It is a reasonable default and you should use it when nothing argues otherwise, but "otherwise" is common:

SituationWhich error costs moreSensible α
Drug safety screeningType II — missing a genuine harm signal is catastrophicLoose, e.g. 0.10 or higher, so that real signals are caught even at the cost of investigating false ones

⚠️ The rule that keeps α honest

α is chosen before the data are seen, and it is not revised afterwards. "We used 0.10 because the result was p = 0.08" is the same offence as post-hoc tail selection: it converts a decision rule into a description of what you found. State α in the method section, not the results section.

🏁 30-second recap

Type I = false alarm, probability α, chosen by you. Type II = missed effect, probability β, a consequence of α, n, variability and the true effect size. Power = 1 − β. The two trade against each other along the decision line; only a larger n improves both. β depends on which alternative is true, and α + β is not 1.

6.4The test statistic and the critical-value method

Two routes lead to the same verdict. This section is the older one, built for a world of printed tables; §6.5 is the one your software will use. You need both, because exams ask for the first and reports quote the second.

Every test statistic has the same shape

test statistic  =  ( estimate − claimed value )  ÷  standard error

Read it aloud: how far the data fell from H₀, measured in standard errors. A value of −4.0 means the sample mean landed four standard errors below what H₀ predicted. A value of 0.6 means it landed comfortably within the ordinary scatter. That is the entire content of a test statistic, and the four formulas in this chapter differ only in what goes in the denominator.

The five steps, worked through once

☎️ Worked example — is the BPO under its SLA?

A service-level agreement caps average handle time at 330 seconds. Historical process data give σ = 45 s. A random sample of 100 calls this month averages 312 s. Test at α = 0.05 whether the process is genuinely under the cap.

Step 1 · state the hypotheses H₀: μ = 330     H₁: μ < 330   (left-tailed — only being under the cap would change the conclusion) Step 2 · choose α and find the critical value α = 0.05, left tail → zcrit = −1.645. Reject H₀ if z < −1.645. Step 3 · compute the test statistic SE = σ/√n = 45/√100 = 4.50 z = (x̄ − μ₀)/SE = (312 − 330)/4.50 = −18/4.50 = −4.00 Step 4 · compare and decide −4.00 < −1.645 → the statistic falls in the rejection region → reject H₀ Step 5 · state the conclusion in context "At the 5% significance level there is sufficient evidence that mean handle time is below the 330-second SLA cap."
📝 What a conclusion should and should not say

Good: "There is sufficient evidence at α = 0.05 to conclude that mean handle time is below 330 seconds (z = −4.00, p < 0.001, n = 100)."

Bad: "We proved handle time is 312 seconds." The test says nothing about 312 being the true mean; it says 330 is implausible.

Also bad: "The result was significant." Significant at what level, on what parameter, in which direction, from how many observations? A conclusion that a non-statistician cannot act on has not finished the job.

The rejection region, and an equivalent way to see it

Instead of converting the data to a z and comparing with −1.645, you can convert the critical value back to the original units and compare directly:

critical x̄ = μ₀ + zcrit·SE = 330 + (−1.645)(4.50) = 322.60 seconds Observed x̄ = 312 < 322.60 → reject H₀  (the same verdict, reached in seconds)

This version is often better in a meeting, because 322.60 is a number an operations manager can hold in mind: any monthly sample averaging below 322.6 seconds is evidence of genuine improvement; anything above it is within normal variation. That is a decision rule the business can adopt, and it is the same object as Figure 6.4's decision line.

🏁 30-second recap

Test statistic = (estimate − claimed value) ÷ standard error. Five steps: hypotheses → α and critical value → statistic → compare → conclusion in context. Worked case: SE 4.50, z = −4.00 against a critical −1.645, so reject H₀. The critical value can also be expressed in the original units (x̄ = 322.60 s), which is usually the more useful form for a manager.

6.5The p-value method — and what a p-value is not

The critical-value method answers "is the statistic beyond the line?" The p-value method answers "how far beyond, expressed as a probability?" It is strictly more informative, it is what every piece of software reports, and it is misunderstood by a large majority of the people who quote it.

📖 The definition, worth memorising word for word

The p-value is the probability of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, computed assuming the null hypothesis is true.

Three parts of that sentence do work. "At least as extreme" means it is a tail area, not a point probability. "As the one observed" means the boundary is set by your data rather than by α. "Assuming H₀ is true" is the conditional that everything else in this section turns on.

🔁 The decision rule, and why the two methods never disagree
p < α  →  reject H₀   ·   p ≥ α  →  fail to reject H₀

The critical value is the statistic whose tail area is exactly α. So a statistic beyond the critical value necessarily has a tail area below α, and vice versa. They are the same comparison — one made on the horizontal axis, the other on the area. If you ever get different verdicts from the two routes, you have made an arithmetic error, most often by forgetting to double the tail for a two-sided test.

Why the p-value method is preferred in practice

📊 It carries more information

"Reject at α = 0.05" is the same verdict whether p = 0.049 or p = 0.000001. The p-value lets the reader see the difference between "barely" and "overwhelmingly", which usually matters.

👥 It lets the reader choose α

A reader who would have used α = 0.01 can apply their own threshold to your reported p. A bare "significant at 5%" forces your judgement on them.

💻 It matches the software

Every statistical package reports p-values, not critical values. Fluency with p is what makes the output readable.

The four misreadings

The misreadingWhy it is wrongHow to catch it
"p = 0.03, so there is a 3% chance H₀ is true"Inverts the conditional. The p-value is P(data | H₀), not P(H₀ | data). Those are different quantities, exactly as P(wet | raining) differs from P(raining | wet).Read the definition again: everything is computed assuming H₀ is true. A calculation that assumes H₀ cannot then report the probability that H₀ holds.
"p = 0.03, so there is a 97% chance H₁ is true"The same error with the complement taken. H₁ is not even a single hypothesis — it covers every value except μ₀ — so there is no one probability to report.Ask which specific alternative the 97% is supposed to describe. There is no answer.
"p is tiny, so the effect is large"p depends on the effect and on n. A trivial effect with a huge sample gives a minuscule p. If a report gives only a p, it has told you whether, not how much.

🚫 And the boundary is not a cliff

p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 are, as evidence, essentially identical. Treating one as a discovery and the other as a non-result is an artefact of a threshold chosen for typographical convenience a century ago. Report the actual p-value, report the interval, and let the reader see that a borderline case is borderline. The professional habit is to write "p = 0.051" rather than "not significant", and "p = 0.049" rather than "significant" — the numbers say more than the labels.

🏁 30-second recap

The p-value is P(data at least this extreme | H₀ true). Reject when p < α. It always agrees with the critical-value method because they measure the same picture two ways. It is not the probability H₀ is true, not the probability H₁ is true, not a measure of effect size, and not a replication rate. And 0.049 versus 0.051 is not a real distinction.

6.6Testing a mean when σ is known: the z-test

The same routing question as Chapter 5. Given σ, use z; given s, use t. Everything else is the five steps.

z  =  ( x̄ − μ₀ )  ÷  ( σ / √n )

Notice that the two-tailed 0.10 value equals the one-tailed 0.05 value, and the two-tailed 0.05 equals the one-tailed 0.025. That is not a coincidence — it is the α-splitting from Figure 6.2, and it is why switching tails after the fact doubles your real error rate.

📦 Worked example — a borderline case

The same BPO now tests against a tighter internal target of 320 seconds rather than the contractual 330. Same data: σ = 45, n = 100, x̄ = 312. Is the process under the internal target?

H₀: μ = 320    H₁: μ < 320    α = 0.05, zcrit = −1.645 z = (312 − 320)/4.50 = −8/4.50 = −1.78 p = P(Z < −1.78) = 0.0377 −1.78 < −1.645, and 0.0377 < 0.05 → reject H₀

Reading it honestly. This is a much weaker result than the first test, and the report should say so. "There is evidence at the 5% level that handle time is below the 320-second internal target (z = −1.78, p = 0.038, n = 100), though the margin is not large — at α = 0.01 the same data would not support the claim." That sentence is more useful to a manager than a bare "significant", and it is the kind of nuance a p-value carries and a critical-value verdict throws away.

⚠️ The conditions, and the one that usually fails
  • Probability sample. Chapter 4's standing precondition, still standing. No formula here can see a biased design.
  • Normal sampling distribution of x̄. Guaranteed if the population is normal; otherwise supplied by the CLT for n ≥ 30, more if the population is heavily skewed.
  • σ genuinely known. Rare — it requires a long-established, tightly monitored process. If the problem hands you s, this is the wrong test.
🏁 30-second recap

z = (x̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n). One-tailed critical values 1.282 / 1.645 / 2.326 at α = 0.10 / 0.05 / 0.01; two-tailed 1.645 / 1.960 / 2.576. Worked case: z = −1.78, p = 0.038 — significant at 5% but not at 1%, and the report should say so rather than just "significant".

6.7Testing a mean when σ is unknown: the t-test

The workhorse, exactly as in Chapter 5. Two substitutions and one extra assumption.

t  =  ( x̄ − μ₀ )  ÷  ( s / √n )     with df = n − 1
🍗 Worked example — the franchise food-cost target, re-read as a test

Chapter 5 estimated chain-wide food cost from 15 branches: x̄ = 32.50%, s = 1.6138, and the franchise agreement targets 33.0%. Head office now asks the sharper question: is the chain on target, or not? Test at α = 0.05.

Step 1 · hypotheses H₀: μ = 33.0    H₁: μ ≠ 33.0   (two-tailed — running well below target is also a problem) Step 2 · α and critical values α = 0.05, df = 15 − 1 = 14 → tcrit = ±2.145 Step 3 · the test statistic SE = s/√n = 1.6138/√15 = 0.41668 t = (32.50 − 33.00)/0.41668 = −0.50/0.41668 = −1.200 Step 4 · compare |−1.200| = 1.200 < 2.145 → fail to reject H₀ p = 2 × P(T14 < −1.200) = 2 × 0.12503 = 0.2501  →  0.2501 > 0.05, same verdict Step 5 · conclusion in context "There is insufficient evidence at the 5% level to conclude that chain-wide food cost differs from the 33% target (t = −1.200, df = 14, p = 0.250)."
🔗 The same answer, from Chapter 5

The 95% confidence interval computed in §5.5 was [31.61%, 33.39%], and 33.0 falls inside it. That is not a coincidence and it is not a second piece of evidence — it is the same calculation. §6.9 makes the equivalence explicit. For now, note that you already knew the verdict before running the test, which is a good reason to compute the interval as well: it tells you what the test tells you, plus the range of values the data cannot rule out.

🚫 What "fail to reject" does not mean here

It does not mean food cost is 33.0%. The interval shows the data are equally compatible with 31.7%, 32.5% and 33.3%. Writing "the test confirms we are on target" is the accept-the-null error, and it is the most common single mistake in this chapter. The defensible sentence is: "this sample provides no evidence of a departure from target" — a statement about the evidence, not about the world.

The assumption that t carries and z did not

At small n the t-test requires the population to be approximately normal, because s must behave predictably for the t distribution to apply. The check is the same as in Chapter 5: sort the data, compare the mean with the median, look for outliers.

29.8   30.4   30.8   31.2   31.5   31.7   32.2   32.6   32.8   33.0   33.5   33.9   34.1   34.8   35.2

Median 32.6 against a mean of 32.50, range spread evenly either side, no stragglers. The t-test is safe to report. At n ≥ 30 the procedure is robust and mild departures stop mattering; at n = 8 from a skewed population it is not trustworthy, and the honest move is to say so rather than to produce a p-value.

🏁 30-second recap

t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n) with df = n − 1. Worked case: SE 0.41668, t = −1.200 against ±2.145, p = 0.250 → fail to reject. That matches §5.5's interval [31.61, 33.39] containing 33.0, because it is the same calculation. Failing to reject is not confirming the target, and small-n t-tests need an approximately normal population.

6.8Testing a proportion

Structurally the same test, with one genuine difference from the confidence interval you built in Chapter 5 — and it is a difference students lose marks on every year.

z  =  ( p̂ − p₀ )  ÷  √[ p₀ ( 1 − p₀ ) / n ]
🔑 The standard error uses p₀, not p̂

Compare the two formulas side by side:

PurposeStandard errorWhy
Confidence interval√[ p̂(1 − p̂)/n ]No value of p is assumed, so the best available estimate of the spread is the one built from the data.
Hypothesis test√[ p₀(1 − p₀)/n ]The whole calculation is performed assuming H₀ is true. If H₀ says p = p₀, then the sampling distribution's spread is determined by p₀ — using p̂ would mean assuming H₀ for the centre and denying it for the spread.

The condition to check also changes accordingly: verify np₀ ≥ 5 and n(1 − p₀) ≥ 5, using the hypothesised value.

💳 Worked example — is the supplier's default rate above contract?

A lender's contract with a loan originator specifies a portfolio default rate of no more than 4%. A random audit of 2,000 loans finds 104 in default. Test at α = 0.05 whether the originator is exceeding the contracted rate.

Step 1 · hypotheses H₀: p = 0.04    H₁: p > 0.04   (right-tailed — only an excess breaches the contract) Step 2 · α, condition and critical value np₀ = 2000(0.04) = 80 ✓    n(1−p₀) = 1,920 ✓    both far above 5 α = 0.05, right tail → zcrit = +1.645 Step 3 · the test statistic p̂ = 104/2000 = 0.052 SE = √[0.04 × 0.96 / 2000] = √0.0000192 = 0.0043818 z = (0.052 − 0.040)/0.0043818 = 0.012/0.0043818 = +2.739 Step 4 · compare 2.739 > 1.645 → reject H₀    p = P(Z > 2.739) = 0.0031 Step 5 · conclusion "There is strong evidence at the 5% level that the default rate exceeds the contracted 4% (z = 2.74, p = 0.003, n = 2,000)."
🧮 What using p̂ would have done

Building the standard error from p̂ = 0.052 gives √[0.052 × 0.948/2000] = 0.0049647, and z = 0.012/0.0049647 = 2.417 with p = 0.0078. Same verdict here, different numbers — and the direction of the error is not fixed. When p̂ sits closer to 0.5 than p₀ does, the wrong standard error is larger and the test is conservative; when p̂ is further from 0.5, the wrong standard error is smaller and the test is anti-conservative, inflating significance. Since you cannot know in advance which way it will run, the rule is simply to use p₀ every time.

✍️ Reflect

The audit found 104 defaults where the contract implies about 80. Suppose the originator replies: "104 against 80 is a difference of 24 loans out of 2,000 — that is 1.2 percentage points, which is commercially trivial." Is that a good reply? Consider what a 1.2-point excess costs on a ₱4 billion portfolio, and then consider whether the reply is really about the statistics at all. Statistical significance and commercial materiality are separate questions, and a complete audit answers both.

🏁 30-second recap

z = (p̂ − p₀)/√[p₀(1−p₀)/n]. The standard error uses p₀, because the whole calculation assumes H₀ — this is the one place a test and an interval genuinely differ. Check np₀ ≥ 5 and n(1−p₀) ≥ 5. Worked case: p̂ = 0.052, SE = 0.0043818, z = +2.739, p = 0.003 → reject.

6.9Testing a variance, and the interval–test duality

The fourth test, and then the result that ties the last two chapters together.

χ²  =  ( n − 1 ) s²  ÷  σ₀²     with df = n − 1

The reasoning is the same as §5.8's: a sample variance is not normally distributed, so its reference distribution is chi-square. Because that distribution is right-skewed, the critical values are not symmetric and you must read the correct one for your tail.

🥤 Worked example — is the supplier out of tolerance?

A supplier's contract promises a net-weight standard deviation of no more than 3.0 g. A random sample of 25 packs gives s = 4.1 g; net weights are approximately normal. Test at α = 0.05 whether the supplier is out of tolerance.

Step 1 · hypotheses H₀: σ = 3.0    H₁: σ > 3.0   (right-tailed — only excess variability breaches the contract) Step 2 · α and critical value df = 25 − 1 = 24, α = 0.05 in the upper tail → χ²crit = 36.415 Step 3 · the test statistic χ² = (n − 1)s²/σ₀² = 24 × 4.1² / 3.0² = 24 × 16.81 / 9 = 403.44/9 = 44.827 Step 4 · compare 44.827 > 36.415 → reject H₀    p = P(χ²24 > 44.827) = 0.0061 Note it also clears the 1% cut-off of 42.980, so the finding survives a stricter α. Step 5 · conclusion "There is strong evidence that the process standard deviation exceeds the contracted 3.0 g (χ² = 44.83, df = 24, p = 0.006). Contractual action is warranted."
⚠️ The strictest assumption in the chapter, again

The chi-square test requires the population to be normally distributed, and it is not robust to departures. A large n does not rescue it — the Central Limit Theorem applies to sample means, not to sample variances. With skewed or heavy-tailed data this test can be badly wrong at any sample size, and the modern remedy is a bootstrap or a distribution-free alternative. The case above states normality explicitly for exactly this reason; if the case had not, the correct answer would begin by asking for the histogram.

The duality: a two-sided test and a confidence interval are one calculation

Look back at what has happened twice now. The food-cost target of 33.0 fell inside §5.5's interval, and §6.7's two-sided test failed to reject it. The supplier's 3.0 g fell outside §5.8's interval for σ, and a test rejected it. That is not luck — with one caution. Both intervals are two-sided, so the exact partner of each is a two-sided test. The food-cost test above was two-sided, so it pairs directly. The variance test above was one-tailed (p = 0.0061); its two-sided counterpart gives p = 0.0122 and also rejects, and that is the version the two-sided interval [3.201, 5.704] corresponds to.

📐 Why the equivalence must hold

The 95% interval is the set of values x̄ ± t·SE. A two-sided test rejects μ₀ when |x̄ − μ₀| > t·SE. Rearranged, that says the test rejects exactly when μ₀ lies outside x̄ ± t·SE — which is the interval. So:

a 100(1−α)% interval is precisely the set of values that a two-sided test at α would not reject

That is a genuinely useful way to think about a confidence interval: it is not merely a range, it is the list of null hypotheses your data are compatible with. Which immediately explains why "fail to reject" cannot mean "H₀ is true" — the interval is full of other values the test would equally have failed to reject.

🔀 Two qualifications worth knowing

One-tailed tests do not match a two-sided interval. A one-tailed test at α = 0.05 corresponds to a one-sided 95% bound, or equivalently to a two-sided 90% interval. Comparing a one-tailed test with a standard two-sided interval will occasionally give apparently contradictory answers, and the contradiction is in the comparison, not in the statistics.

The proportion case is approximate. Because the test uses p₀ in the standard error and the interval uses p̂, the two are not algebraically identical for a proportion. They agree in the overwhelming majority of cases and can disagree when p̂ is close to a boundary of the interval. Everywhere else in this course, the duality is exact.

Which to report

⚖️ Report the test when

Someone has already put a number on the table — a contract, a specification, a target, a competitor's claim — and the decision is binary. "Is the supplier compliant?" wants a verdict.

📏 Report the interval when

The magnitude matters, or nobody has named a value yet. "How bad is the supplier?" wants [3.20, 5.70] g, which a p-value cannot supply.

The professional answer is nearly always both: "σ is estimated at 4.1 g, 95% CI [3.20, 5.70], which exceeds the contracted 3.0 g (one-tailed χ² test: χ² = 44.83, df = 24, p = 0.006)." A dozen extra words, and the reader gets the verdict, the magnitude and the uncertainty at once — with the tail stated, so nobody has to guess which interval it pairs with.

🏁 30-second recap

χ² = (n−1)s²/σ₀² with df = n − 1; read the correct asymmetric critical value. Worked case: χ² = 44.827 against 36.415, p = 0.006 → reject. A 100(1−α)% interval is the set of null values a two-sided α-level test would not reject, so the two never disagree — with the qualifications that one-tailed tests match one-sided bounds, and that the proportion case is approximate.

6.10Power, effect size, and the five ways tests get abused

Everything so far has been about controlling false alarms. This section is about the other error — and about the fact that a test which controls α perfectly can still be worthless, misleading, or both.

Power: could this study have found what it was looking for?

power  =  1 − β  =  P( reject H₀ | H₀ is false by a specified amount )

Power is not a property of a test in the abstract. It is the answer to a specific question: if the true mean really were 320 rather than 330, how often would a study like this one notice? Change the effect size, the sample size or α, and the answer changes.

🔋 Worked example — a study that could not have succeeded

A bank pilots a new branch service model at 16 branches. Historical NPS has σ = 12 points and the current mean is 42. The pilot averages 45. Management tests H₀: μ = 42 against H₁: μ > 42 at α = 0.05.

SE = 12/√16 = 3.00, so z = (45 − 42)/3.00 = +1.00, p = 0.159. Fail to reject. The memo concludes: "the new model produced no improvement."

The question the memo never asked: could it have detected one? Critical x̄ = 42 + 1.645(3.00) = 46.93 — the pilot needed a 4.93-point lift to reach significance If the true improvement were 3 points (μ = 45): power = P(x̄ > 46.93 | μ = 45) = 0.26 If the true improvement were 5 points (μ = 47): power = 0.51 A genuine 3-point improvement would have been missed three times in four. The study was designed to fail. What n would have been needed? n = [ (zcrit + zβ) σ / δ ]², with zcrit = zα = 1.645 because this test is one-tailed = [ (1.645 + 0.842)(12) / 3 ]² = (9.948)² = 98.9 → n = 99 for 80% power For 90% power: [ (1.645 + 1.282)(12)/3 ]² = 137.0 → n = 138 Had the test been two-tailed, zcrit would be zα/2 = 1.960 and the requirement would rise to [(1.960 + 0.842)(12)/3]² = 125.6 → n = 126. Using the one-tailed multiplier for a two-tailed design under-sizes the study by about 20%.
🚫 "No significant difference" ≠ "no difference"

The memo's conclusion is not merely unsupported, it is uninterpretable. A study with 26% power fails to reject almost everything, so its failure to reject carries essentially no information. The honest sentence is: "the pilot did not detect a significant improvement, but with n = 16 it had only a 26% chance of detecting a 3-point lift, so this result should not be read as evidence against the new model." Then either scale the pilot to n ≈ 99 or stop running it. Always ask for the power calculation before accepting a null result — and note that this is a question to ask at the design stage, because by the time the data are in, the answer cannot be changed.

Effect size: how big, not just whether

A test answers a yes/no question. It does not, on its own, say whether the difference is worth acting on — and with a large enough sample, it will call any difference significant.

📏 Cohen's d — the standard measure
d  =  ( x̄ − μ₀ ) ÷ s

The difference expressed in standard deviations, so it is comparable across studies and unaffected by n. The conventional benchmarks are 0.2 small · 0.5 medium · 0.8 large, and they are rough guides rather than laws — a "small" effect on a metric that drives millions of pesos may be the most important finding in the report.

For the two studies above: Study A's d = 12/45 = 0.267; Study B's d = 0.3/45 = 0.007. The arithmetic is unambiguous about which effect is larger, and it is the opposite of what the p-values suggest.

🧮 The number that settles it

Effect sizes become decisions when you price them. Study B's 0.3-second saving across 250,000 calls is 75,000 seconds, or about 21 agent-hours a year. Against a contact-centre payroll that is a rounding error, and no amount of statistical significance changes that. Always translate the effect into the unit the business actually cares about — pesos, hours, customers, defects — before deciding whether a significant result deserves a decision.

🛡️ The Bonferroni correction, and its cost

The simplest fix is to divide α by the number of tests. Twenty tests at a family-wide 5% means each is judged at 0.05/20 = 0.0025. A subgroup that came back at p = 0.03 is then nowhere near significant, which is the correct reading — it is exactly what noise produces.

The correction is deliberately conservative, and its cost is power: real effects in the other nineteen subgroups become much harder to detect. More refined procedures exist (Holm's step-down method, and the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate control that is standard in genomics), but the discipline matters more than the method. The honest alternative, when no correction is applied, is to label the finding exploratory and to confirm it in a fresh sample before anyone acts on it.

🧾 The reporting checklist for any test
  • The hypotheses, with the tail and the reason for it.
  • α, chosen in advance, and any correction applied for multiple testing.
  • The test statistic, its degrees of freedom, and the exact p-value — not "p < 0.05".
  • The effect size and a confidence interval, in the units the business uses.
  • The sample size and the design, because non-sampling error is invisible to every formula here.
  • For a null result, the power to detect an effect worth detecting.

🚦 The decision cards

✍️ Where the equals sign goes

Always in H₀. The test builds a sampling distribution around one specific value, and only H₀ supplies one. Whatever you are trying to establish goes in H₁, which carries the burden of proof — which is why a test can support H₁ but never support H₀.

🧭 Which tail

Ask: what would I need to see in order to act? Only an increase → right. Only a decrease → left. Either departure matters → two. Decide before seeing the data, and justify it from the business question, not from the result.

🚫 Never "accept H₀"

Failing to reject means the data are compatible with H₀ — and with every other value in the confidence interval. Write "insufficient evidence to conclude…", never "we confirmed that…".

🔑 p₀ or p̂?

Test → p₀. The calculation assumes H₀, so the spread must come from H₀ too. Interval → p̂. No value is assumed, so the data supply the spread. Getting this backwards is the signature error of §6.8.

🔋 Before believing a null result

Ask for the power. Below about 0.80 for an effect worth detecting, "no significant difference" carries essentially no information — the study could not have found one.

📏 Significance ≠ importance

A large enough n makes any difference significant. Report the effect size and the confidence interval beside every p-value, and price the effect in pesos, hours or customers before recommending action.

✅ Model answer

B · Post-hoc tail selection — the conclusion does not survive.

The review states plainly that a two-tailed test was planned, and then reports a one-tailed p-value. The arithmetic of the switch is correct — a one-tailed p is exactly half a two-tailed one — but the switch itself is not.

Condition: np₀ = 1600(0.12) = 192 ✓   n(1−p₀) = 1,408 ✓ SE = √[0.12 × 0.88 / 1600] = √0.000066 = 0.0081240 z = (0.135 − 0.120)/0.0081240 = 1.846 Two-tailed p = 2 × P(Z > 1.846) = 2 × 0.03242 = 0.0648  →  fail to reject at α = 0.05

Why the switch is invalid. A one-tailed test is legitimate only when the tail was fixed before the data were seen. Here the team would plainly have used the left tail had conversion fallen, so its rejection region effectively covers both tails at 5% each — a true Type I error rate of 10%, not 5%. The reported 0.0324 therefore understates the false-alarm risk by a factor of two, and the correct conclusion is that the campaign fell short of significance.

Consequence. The bank would scale a campaign on evidence that does not meet its own stated threshold. Note the honest reading of 0.0648, which is not "the campaign did nothing": the point estimate is a 1.5-percentage-point lift, and the data are simply not decisive. The right recommendation is to extend the test rather than to declare victory — at n = 1,600 the study is close, and roughly doubling it would resolve the question either way.

C · The wrong standard error — the conclusion survives.

The analyst built the standard error from p̂ = 0.052. That is the confidence-interval formula. A test is computed assuming H₀ is true, so if H₀ says p = 0.04, the sampling distribution's spread must be built from 0.04 as well — using p̂ would mean assuming H₀ for the centre and denying it for the spread.

Condition: np₀ = 2000(0.04) = 80 ✓   n(1−p₀) = 1,920 ✓ Correct SE = √[0.04 × 0.96 / 2000] = √0.0000192 = 0.0043818  (analyst used 0.0049647) Correct z = (0.052 − 0.040)/0.0043818 = 2.739  (analyst reported 2.417) Correct p = P(Z > 2.739) = 0.0031  (analyst reported 0.0078)

Does the conclusion change? No — both p-values are below 0.05 and indeed below 0.01, so the partner is found to exceed the ceiling either way. But the answer "it made no difference" is incomplete and should not earn full marks. The direction of the error is not fixed: here p̂ = 0.052 sits closer to 0.5 than p₀ = 0.04 does, so p̂(1−p̂) is larger, the wrong standard error is larger, and the test was made conservative. Had p̂ fallen on the other side of p₀ — or had the parameter been, say, a 90% satisfaction rate — the same mistake would have made the standard error smaller and the test anti-conservative, inflating significance. Since you cannot know the direction in advance, the rule is simply to use p₀ every time.

Consequence. In this instance, none — but the review's credibility is at stake, because a counterparty's own analysts will recompute the test and get a different z. Note also what the audit found in substance: 104 defaults against the 80 the contract implies, an excess of 24 loans and 1.2 percentage points. On a large book that is material, and the complete report gives both the test and the 95% interval for the true rate, [4.23%, 6.17%] — the entire interval sits above the 4% ceiling, which is the more persuasive way to put it to a partner.

Essay 2 · 25 points · Analyse & Evaluate
Audit items A and F, both of which report a null result. For each, explain precisely why the stated conclusion is unsafe, compute what the study could actually have detected, and state what the bank should do instead. Then explain, in language a non-statistical board member would follow, why "we found no significant difference" and "there is no difference" are different statements.
💡 Before you look — what a strong answer must contain
  • The accept-the-null error named explicitly in item A, with the confidence interval used as the argument.
  • A power calculation for item F, against a stated effect size.
  • The sample size that would have been required, computed.
  • Recognition that A and F are not the same error, though both involve a null result.
  • A plain-language explanation that does not use the word "null".
✅ Model answer

A · Accepting the null — the conclusion asserts more than the data can carry.

The test is correctly executed. σ is unknown and s = 1.5 was computed from the sample, so a t-test with df = 39 is right, and p = 0.0997 > 0.05 means H₀ is not rejected. The error is entirely in the sentence that follows: "completion time is 8.0 minutes".

SE = s/√n = 1.5/√40 = 1.5/6.3246 = 0.23717 t = (7.6 − 8.0)/0.23717 = −1.687, df = 39, two-tailed p = 0.0997 t0.025, 39 = 2.023, so the 95% interval is 7.6 ± 2.023(0.23717) = [7.12, 8.08] minutes

The interval is the argument. It contains 8.0 — which is why the test did not reject — but it also contains 7.2, 7.5 and 7.9. Every one of those values would equally have survived the test. So failing to reject 8.0 does not single it out; it merely fails to rule it out, along with a range of genuinely different outcomes. A redesign that cut onboarding from 8.0 to 7.2 minutes would be a real 10% improvement, and this study is entirely compatible with it.

What the bank should do. Report the estimate and the interval, not the verdict: "mean completion time is estimated at 7.6 minutes (95% CI 7.12 to 8.08), a point estimate 0.4 minutes below the previous standard, but the study cannot distinguish that from no change." Then decide whether 0.4 minutes is worth resolving. If it is, note that detecting a 0.5-minute change with 80% power in a two-sided test would need n = [(zα/2 + zβ)s/δ]² = [(1.960 + 0.842)(1.5)/0.5]² = 70.6 → about n = 71 — under twice the current sample, and cheap. Note the multiplier: a two-sided design uses zα/2 = 1.960, not the 1.645 a one-tailed design would use.

F · An underpowered study read as evidence of absence — a different and worse error.

Item A at least had reasonable precision. Item F does not, and the distinction matters: here the null result carries almost no information at all.

SE = σ/√n = 12/√20 = 2.683 z = (45.5 − 42)/2.683 = 1.304, right-tailed p = 0.096 → fail to reject ✓ (the arithmetic is right) The question the review never asked Critical x̄ = 42 + 1.645(2.683) = 46.41 — the pilot needed a 4.41-point lift to reach significance If the true improvement were the 3.5 points observed: power = P(x̄ > 46.41 | μ = 45.5) = 0.37 Even a 6-point improvement (μ = 48) would have been detected only 72% of the time A genuine 3.5-point improvement would have been missed almost two times in three. The pilot was designed to fail. What would have been needed n = [ (zcrit + zβ) σ / δ ]², with zcrit = zα = 1.645 because this test is one-tailed = [(1.645 + 0.842)(12)/3.5]² = (8.526)² = 72.7 → n = 73 for 80% power For 90% power: n = 101

What the bank should do. Not discontinue. The recommendation "discontinue the pilot" treats a failure to detect as a finding of no effect, and on this evidence it is closer to a coin flip. The defensible options are to extend the pilot to about 73 branches — the marginal cost of 53 more branches against a decision affecting the whole network is small — or to stop for reasons of cost or strategy while stating clearly that the pilot produced no usable evidence either way. What the bank must not do is record "the service model does not work" in the minutes, because the study never had the sensitivity to establish that.

🗣️ For the board, without the word "null"

"Imagine looking for a coin on a dark floor with a weak torch. If you sweep the room and find nothing, there are two possible explanations: there is no coin, or your torch is not bright enough. Study F used a torch bright enough to find the coin only about a third of the time. So 'we did not find an improvement' is, in that study, almost the same sentence as 'we were not really looking'. Before we treat a negative result as a finding, we should always ask how bright the torch was — and that is a question to settle when we design the study, not after the data are in."

🔍 Why A and F are not the same error

Both report a null result, but the diagnosis differs. A is a reasonably precise study whose conclusion overreaches the data — the fix is to restate the finding as an interval. F is a study with so little sensitivity that its result is nearly uninformative — the fix is a bigger study or none at all. A first-class answer distinguishes the two rather than treating both as "accepting the null", because the recommended actions are different.

Essay 3 · 25 points · Evaluate & Create
Audit items D and E. For each, explain why a technically correct calculation supports an indefensible decision, quantify the problem, and state what the bank should do. Then draft the analytics unit's revised reporting standard — the rules that would have prevented all six failures — and recommend what the board should do about the ₱18 million script rollout.
💡 Before you look — what a strong answer must contain
  • For D, the effect size computed and translated into a business unit.
  • Recognition that D's arithmetic is entirely correct — the failure is one of judgement.
  • For E, the family-wide error rate computed, and the corrected threshold.
  • A reporting standard with rules that map onto all six failures, not a generic list of good practice.
  • A recommendation on the ₱18 million with a stated basis.
✅ Model answer

D · Significance mistaken for importance — a correct test supporting a wrong decision.

Nothing in this calculation is wrong. σ is known, n is enormous, and the test is executed properly. The failure is that the review stops at the p-value.

SE = 45/√250,000 = 45/500 = 0.09 s z = (329.7 − 330)/0.09 = −3.333, two-tailed p = 0.00086 ✓ 95% CI for μ = 329.7 ± 1.96(0.09) = [329.52, 329.88] seconds Effect size: Cohen's d = 0.3/45 = 0.007 — roughly thirty times below the "small" benchmark of 0.2 Now price it 0.3 s × 250,000 calls = 75,000 seconds = 20.8 agent-hours per year ₱18 million to buy roughly 21 agent-hours a year.

The interval is the clearest way to make the point to a board: the true mean lies between 329.52 and 329.88 seconds. The bank is being asked to spend ₱18 million on a difference the interval itself pins at under half a second. With n = 250,000, a difference of any size at all would have been significant — the p-value is measuring the sample size as much as the effect. The recommendation is to decline the rollout, and to note that the study's real finding is a useful one: the new script makes no material difference, so the money is better spent elsewhere.

E · Multiplicity — a finding that noise alone would produce.

P(at least one false positive) = 1 − 0.95²⁰ = 1 − 0.3585 = 0.6415 Expected number of "significant" segments under a universal null = 20 × 0.05 = 1.0 Bonferroni threshold = 0.05/20 = 0.0025    observed p = 0.03 → not significant

If the campaign had no effect in any segment, the expected number of segments returning p < 0.05 is exactly one — which is precisely what the review found. Finding one significant segment among twenty is therefore the modal outcome of a study in which nothing is happening, and reporting it as a discovery is close to reporting the absence of a finding as a finding. Against the corrected threshold of 0.0025 the result is twelve times too large.

What the bank should do. Label the segment result exploratory, and confirm it in a fresh sample before reallocating any budget. That is not merely a formality: a confirmatory test on new data has no multiplicity problem, and if the segment effect is real it will survive. Note also that Bonferroni is deliberately conservative and costs power in the other nineteen segments, so a bank running many segment analyses routinely should consider a false-discovery-rate procedure rather than applying Bonferroni indiscriminately. The discipline matters more than the specific correction.

The revised reporting standard. Six rules, one per failure, each stated so that compliance is checkable:

  1. Hypotheses, α and tails are recorded before data collection begins, with the tail justified from the business question. Any deviation is reported as a deviation. (prevents B)
  2. Every test reports the statistic, its degrees of freedom and the exact p-value — never "p < 0.05" — together with the standard error used and the formula it came from. (prevents C)
  3. Every test is accompanied by an effect size and a confidence interval in business units — pesos, minutes, customers — and by a sentence stating whether the effect is material. (prevents D)
  4. The number of tests run is stated, and any result from a family of more than one carries either a correction or an explicit "exploratory" label. (prevents E)
  5. No null result is reported without its power against a pre-specified effect worth detecting. Below 0.80, the study is reported as inconclusive rather than negative. (prevents F)
  6. The phrases "accept the null", "proves", and "there is no difference" are prohibited in favour of "insufficient evidence", "estimated at", and "the data cannot distinguish". (prevents A)

A seventh rule is worth adding even though no item in this review breaches it: the sample size is fixed in advance from a power calculation and the analysis is run once. Checking a running A/B test daily and stopping at the first crossing of 0.05 is the same multiplicity problem as item E, and it is the failure this review would have produced next quarter.

The recommendation on the ₱18 million. Do not proceed. The evidence for the script change is a difference of 0.3 seconds with a 95% interval of [329.52, 329.88] — the study has established, with unusual precision, that the effect is negligible. That is a genuinely valuable finding and should be reported as such rather than buried: a study this large is well placed to rule out a material effect, and it has done so. The board should be told that the analysis was executed correctly and that the correct reading of it argues against the expenditure.

If the board wishes to proceed for reasons outside this analysis — brand consistency, agent preference, regulatory language — that is a legitimate decision, and the minutes should record that it was made on those grounds and not on the statistical evidence. Distinguishing "the analysis supports this" from "the analysis does not oppose this" is exactly the discipline the revised standard exists to enforce.

🎯 What separates a first-class answer

Anyone can compute 1 − 0.95²⁰. A first-class answer notices that item D contains no statistical error at all — the failure is entirely one of judgement, and the correct response praises the analysis while rejecting the recommendation. It prices the effect in agent-hours rather than describing it as "small". It maps each rule of the reporting standard onto a specific failure rather than listing good practice generically. And it separates what the evidence supports from what the board may decide for other reasons.
